## 0. Why does imitation learning need its own field?

Picture teaching someone to drive by having them watch you drive for an afternoon, then putting them alone behind the wheel in traffic. Everything they saw was *you* driving well — they never saw what to do the moment the car drifted half a lane off-center, because you never let it drift that far. If they make one small steering error, they're suddenly in a situation nothing in their afternoon of watching prepared them for.

That is, in miniature, the central problem of **imitation learning (IL)**: training a controller (for a robot, a simulated agent, a self-driving car) to reproduce the behavior of an expert **demonstrator**, using only recorded examples of the expert acting — no hand-written rule for "what to do in each situation," and (in the simplest version) no live guidance during training either.

A few pieces of vocabulary the rest of these notes lean on immediately:

- A **[robot policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_control)** is the function that decides what a robot should do next. It takes in whatever the robot currently senses — an **observation** (camera images, joint sensor readings, etc.) — and outputs an **action** (a motor command). "Training a policy" means fitting this function's parameters (in this course, almost always a neural network's weights) so it produces good actions.
- An **[expert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imitation_learning)** or **demonstrator** is whoever/whatever generated the training data: a human physically operating the robot, a human driving a car, or even a slow, expensive planning algorithm you'd like to replace with a fast learned policy.
- A **demonstration** or **trajectory** is one recorded run: a sequence of (observation, action) pairs from start to finish of a task.
- The **[Markov Decision Process (MDP)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_decision_process)** is the standard mathematical model this whole course sits on top of: an agent occupies a **state**, takes an **action**, the world responds with a new state (according to some transition rule) and (in the reinforcement-learning framing) a **reward**. A **[rollout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_decision_process)** is one full run of a policy through an MDP, start to finish. The **horizon**, written **H** or **T** throughout this literature, is the number of decision steps in one rollout/episode — e.g. a 10-second task controlled at 10 actions/second has horizon 100.
- Imitation learning normally trains on states/actions only, with **no reward signal** — this is what distinguishes it from reinforcement learning (RL), covered starting Week 2. (Some later methods, like the "cost-to-go" idea in §4 and the inverse-reinforcement-learning unit in Week 6, blur this line by inferring a reward/cost from demonstrations — but the base case in this lecture assumes none is given.)

The lecture frames imitation learning for robotics around three "guiding principles": robots shouldn't have to learn every skill from scratch through blind trial-and-error; humans should be able to hand a robot new skills by *demonstrating* them rather than writing new code; and robots should be able to learn by watching *others* — other robots, other people's demonstrations, prior experience — not exclusively their own.

**Why this needs its own theory**, in one sentence, anticipating §3: the driving-instructor scenario above is not standard supervised learning, because the *policy's own errors change what situations it sees next* — and untangling exactly what that does to error, and how to fix it, is what most of this week's readings are about.

## 1. Basic robotics vocabulary this course assumes you don't already know

Before touching any algorithm, a short primer on ordinary hardware terms this course uses constantly. None of these are "obvious" just because they sound like everyday words — treat them the way you'd treat any new technical vocabulary.

| **[End-effector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_end_effector)** | Whatever's at the very tip of the arm doing the actual work — a hand, a suction cup, a welding torch. | The device mounted at the distal end of a manipulator that interacts with the environment. |

| **Kinesthetic demonstration** | Literally grabbing the robot's arm with your hands and physically guiding it through a motion, with the robot recording its own joint sensors as you move it. | A demonstration-collection method where a human directly, physically moves the robot (as opposed to teleoperation through a separate controller device). |
| **Embodiment** | The specific physical "body" a policy is controlling — a robot with a 6-DOF arm and a two-finger gripper is a different embodiment from a humanoid with two 7-DOF arms and five-fingered hands, even doing the "same" task. | The physical form (kinematics, sensors, actuators) that grounds a policy's action space. A recurring theme in §6: whether data/policies collected on one embodiment transfer to another. |
| **Bimanual manipulation** | Using two arms together, the way you'd use both hands to open a jar. | Manipulation tasks/systems involving coordinated control of two robot arms simultaneously. |

## 2. The starting point: behavioral cloning

### 2.1 A non-learned contrast: teach-and-repeat navigation

Before defining behavioral cloning itself, it's worth seeing what "having a robot repeat a route a human showed it" looks like *without* any learned function approximator at all — so that when BC is introduced next, it's clear exactly what work the word "learning" is doing.

Picture a hiking guide who walks a trail once, taking a photo every few steps and jotting down landmarks, then on a later trip simply compares what they currently see against those old photos to check they're still on the same trail, correcting course whenever the current view drifts from the corresponding old photo. Nothing about this requires the guide to have learned what trails *in general* look like — they've just recorded one specific route and are matching against the recording. **[Teach-and-repeat navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robot_navigation)** is the robotics version of exactly this: a robot is driven once, manually, along a route (the "teach" pass), building a map tied to that specific route as it goes; later, it "repeats" the route autonomously by matching its current sensor view against the stored map and correcting its path to stay on it.

This is deliberately **not** one of imitation learning's "demonstration → trained policy" pipelines from §0: there is no dataset of (observation, action) pairs, no network trained by gradient descent, and no loss function minimized over held-out data — the resulting "policy," if you can even call it that, has learned nothing generalizable; it can only retrace the one route it was shown, and would have no idea what to do anywhere else. It sits at the opposite end of a spectrum from ALVINN, immediately below: teach-and-repeat satisfies a robot's need to reproduce one human-demonstrated route with zero learning, while ALVINN generalizes to a whole family of positions, headings, and lighting conditions the demonstrator never exactly showed it, by actually fitting a function to demonstrated data. Keeping this contrast in view up front makes ALVINN's real contribution, and the failure modes (§3) that come with it, much sharper to see.

### Paper Breakdown: Teach-and-Repeat Visual Path Following (Furgale & Barfoot)

*Citation: Paul Furgale, Timothy D. Barfoot, "Visual Path Following on a Manifold in Unstructured, Three-Dimensional Terrain," ICRA 2010.*

**Problem/motivation.** In GPS-denied environments (indoors, under tree canopy, on other planets), a rover's estimate of its own position drifts without bound as it travels, unless something periodically corrects it against the outside world. Prior teach-and-repeat systems had only been demonstrated on flat, planar ground; this paper's stated gap is that "none of the teach-and-repeat algorithms published to date have been tested in unstructured, highly three-dimensional environments" — terrain that climbs, banks, and tilts, where a route can't be treated as living in one flat plane.

**Key idea/innovation.** Build the taught route as a chain of small, overlapping 3D "submaps" — each a stereo-camera feature map plus a locally-fit ground plane, covering at most 5 meters of route — rather than one single global 3D reconstruction of the whole path. This lets following the route reduce, locally, to an easy 2D path-tracking problem inside each submap's own ground plane, even while the overall route moves through genuinely three-dimensional terrain.

**Method & repeat procedure.** There is no training in the neural-network sense anywhere in this pipeline — no loss function, no gradient descent, no learned parameters. Every step below is classical geometric estimation and control, run entirely on hand-designed machinery. *Teach pass:* a human pilots the rover once along the desired route; the system extracts [SURF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speeded_up_robust_features) (Speeded-Up Robust Features — a hand-designed, non-learned image-keypoint descriptor, not anything fit to data) keypoints from stereo camera images, groups the route into a sequence of overlapping submaps (50% overlap between consecutive submaps), and fits a local ground plane per submap via a weighted least-squares/eigenvalue calculation over the tracked 3D feature points. *Repeat pass:* only one submap is held in memory at a time; the rover estimates its own pose via stereo visual odometry plus periodic re-localization against the loaded submap's stored features, projects that 3D pose down onto the submap's local 2D ground plane, and feeds the resulting 2D position/heading error into a classical unicycle-model path-tracking controller (again, not learned) that steers the rover back toward the taught path; the system hands off to the next submap once the rover passes the midpoint of the current one.

**Results.** Tested on a six-wheeled rover with a stereo camera as its only navigation sensor, across routes at the University of Toronto and on Arctic, Mars-analog terrain on Devon Island. Headline number: "out of 32.919 kilometres traveled, only 0.128 kilometres were piloted manually, an autonomy rate of 99.6%." On a three-dimensional test route (camera pitch/roll up to 27°), the route was "taught twice and repeated 12 times," with "every repeat pass... successful, despite the three-dimensional motion of the camera."

**How it connects.** This is the non-learned sibling of the same "teach a robot a route by demonstrating it" goal named in §0's guiding principles — it satisfies "let a human hand the robot a skill by demonstrating it, instead of hand-coding it" every bit as much as BC does, but without any of BC's machinery: no fitted function, no generalization beyond the one taught route, and none of §3's failure modes (there is no covariate shift in the §3.2 sense, because the "policy" is never asked to act anywhere it wasn't already precisely shown). ALVINN's contribution, immediately below, is exactly the opposite trade: it buys generalization to situations never exactly demonstrated, at the cost of the covariate-shift and catastrophic-forgetting problems a system with no generalization at all — like this one — simply never runs into.

### 2.2 Behavioral cloning: treating demonstration as supervised learning

The most direct way to turn "a pile of recorded (observation, action) pairs from an expert" into a controller is **[behavioral cloning (BC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imitation_learning#Behavior_Cloning)**: treat it as an ordinary supervised-learning problem. Collect a dataset `{(o_1, a_1), (o_2, a_2), ..., (o_N, a_N)}` of observations paired with the action the expert took at that moment, and train a network `π_θ(o) → a` to predict the expert's action from the observation, using a standard regression or classification loss (you already know how to do this part — it's ordinary supervised training).

That's genuinely all BC is: no environment interaction during training, no reward, no planning — just "fit a function to the expert's (state, action) pairs." Its appeal is exactly its simplicity, and it remains, per the lecture, "one of the simplest machine learning methods to acquire robotic skills in the real world." Its problems (starting in §3) are equally simple to state but surprisingly deep to fix.

### Paper Breakdown: ALVINN — the historical starting point

*Citation: Dean A. Pomerleau, "ALVINN: An Autonomous Land Vehicle in a Neural Network," Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 1 (NeurIPS/NIPS 1989).*

**Problem/motivation.** In the late 1980s, autonomous road-following was built from hand-engineered image-processing pipelines (edge detectors, hand-tuned road-color segmentation, hand-designed steering logic). Pomerleau's complaint: a fixed, hand-tuned pipeline "remains fixed across various driving situations" — every new road type or lighting condition needs new manual engineering. He proposed instead letting a neural network *learn*, from data, which image features matter for steering.

**Key idea/innovation.** Train one small feedforward network, end-to-end via backpropagation, to map a raw camera (and laser range-finder) image directly to a steering command — this is BC, applied to driving, years before the term "behavioral cloning" was standard vocabulary. As the paper puts it, "the data, not the programmer, determines the salient image features."

**Method & training procedure.** The original network: a **30×32-pixel** camera "retina" (960 units) plus an 8×32 laser-range retina (256 units) plus 1 feedback unit (1,217 input units total) → a **29-unit hidden layer** → a **46-unit output layer**, of which 45 units encode steering curvature (a "hill" of activation centered on the correct curvature, not a single spike) and 1 predicts road brightness for the next frame's feedback input. Rather than risk collecting a large, diverse real-driving dataset (logistically hard at the time), Pomerleau trained on **1,200 simulated road images** with randomized position/orientation/lighting, for 40 epochs, using the "Warp" parallel backprop machine — reaching ~90% correct-curvature-within-tolerance on held-out simulated images, then driving the real NAVLAB test vehicle at 0.5 m/s along a 400 m wooded path.

**Results.** On that single test course, ALVINN performed comparably to CMU's best hand-engineered vision system of the time (which drove marginally faster, at 1 m/s, though on faster dedicated hardware). A companion range-finder ablation showed the laser input was largely dispensable for plain road-following.

**How it connects.** ALVINN is the field's founding demonstration that BC *works at all* for real robot control — but its own 1989 paper already flags, as future work, exactly two of the three failure modes §3.1 formalizes: insufficient training variability (§3.1's *narrow state-space coverage from cautious demonstrators* — the human driver never dangerously demonstrated drifting off-course on purpose, so recovery was never in the data to begin with), and the need to teach recovery from mistakes, not just accurate driving. A later, expanded Pomerleau paper (a 1993 book chapter, "Knowledge-Based Training of Artificial Neural Networks for Autonomous Robot Driving") is where the field's two classic BC fixes were actually first demonstrated in detail: **synthetic image augmentation** (each real camera frame was digitally shifted and rotated 14 extra ways, simulating what the road would look like from slightly off-center or off-angle vehicle positions, with the correct steering angle for each synthetic view recomputed geometrically) to manufacture "how do I get back on track" examples the human driver never dangerously demonstrated on purpose, and a **fixed-size replay buffer** (200 patterns, refreshed each cycle to keep the buffer's average steering direction close to straight-ahead) to stop the network from **catastrophically forgetting** how to handle a curve after a long straight stretch of new training data. With both fixes, that later system trained to a usable policy from about **4 minutes** of live human driving. (Course notes should keep these two Pomerleau papers distinct — the 1989 NeurIPS paper this course cites is the simulated-training, dual-retina proof of concept; the shift/rotate-augmentation-plus-buffer numbers usually quoted for ALVINN come from the follow-up work.) These two problems — a demonstrator that naturally never shows recovery, and forgetting old skills as new data streams in — are exactly what §3.1 names formally as *narrow state-space coverage* and *catastrophic forgetting*, with augmentation and the replay buffer as their respective fixes.

### 2.3 BC's lineage in the wild: modern regression and classification successors

ALVINN's core recipe — camera in, steering-relevant signal out, fit by backpropagation — turned out to be less a historical curiosity than a template that later, much more capable systems still follow almost unchanged decades later. Two later papers make this lineage concrete, and are worth contrasting against each other directly: one keeps ALVINN's original framing of steering as something to *regress*, and the other reframes the identical camera-in/steering-out problem as something to *classify* instead.

There's a second, independent architectural axis these two papers also happen to sit on opposite sides of, worth naming explicitly rather than leaving implicit: **pixels → steering** directly, versus **pixels → intermediate representation → steering**. ALVINN and PilotNet (below) are both "pixels → steering" networks in the strict sense — the network's own output *is* the final control signal (a curvature "hill" for ALVINN, an inverse turning radius for PilotNet), with nothing downstream except actuation. Giusti et al. (further below) instead makes the network output a coarser **intermediate representation** — one of three discrete directional classes (left/straight/right) — and hands that off to a separate, hand-designed reactive controller (steering ∝ P(turn-right) − P(turn-left)) that converts it into an actual steering command. The trade-off runs in both directions: a direct pixels-to-steering network needs no hand-designed conversion step and can express any continuous correction, but ties the learned function to exactly one downstream actuation scheme; routing through an intermediate representation decouples "what does the image imply about direction" from "how do I turn that into a control signal," at the cost of throwing away whatever fine-grained continuous information the coarse representation can't encode.

### Paper Breakdown: PilotNet (End-to-End Learning for Self-Driving Cars)

*Citation: Mariusz Bojarski, Davide Del Testa, Daniel Dworakowski, Bernhard Firner, Beat Flepp, Prasoon Goyal, Lawrence D. Jackel, Mathew Monfort, Urs Muller, Jiakai Zhang, Xin Zhang, Jake Zhao, Karol Zieba, "End to End Learning for Self-Driving Cars," arXiv:1604.07316, 2016.*

**Problem/motivation.** Standard self-driving systems of the time were built from separate, hand-engineered modules — lane detection, path planning, control — each requiring hand-designed rules and features. The authors' own earlier prototype, DAVE, had already shown a neural network could steer end-to-end at all, but unreliably: "DAVE's mean distance between crashes was about 20 meters in complex environments." The goal here is a version reliable enough for real public-road testing.

**Key idea/innovation.** Train one convolutional neural network, dubbed PilotNet, to map a single raw front-facing camera frame directly to a steering command, trained end-to-end via backpropagation — exactly ALVINN's recipe, but on real public roads with a real production car instead of a simulated corridor.

**Method & training procedure.** Architecture: a normalization layer, then five convolutional layers (24, 36, 48, 64, 64 filters), then three fully-connected layers (1164, 100, 50, 10 units) down to a single output neuron — about 250,000 trainable parameters in total ("about 27 million connections"). Input is a normalized 66×200-pixel YUV image; the single output is the *inverse turning radius* `1/r` rather than a raw steering angle, chosen specifically to avoid a mathematical singularity at r = ∞ (driving perfectly straight). Training data: three cameras (left, center, right) mounted behind the windshield of a 2016 Lincoln MKZ (and a 2013 Ford Focus), logging video synchronized with the human driver's actual steering angle (tapped from the car's own CAN bus), across "about 72 hours of driving" across varied US locations, weather, and lighting. To manufacture recovery examples — the same problem ALVINN's own follow-up work solved with synthetic shifts (see "How it connects" above) — the left/right camera images and additional synthetic viewpoint shifts/rotations are relabeled with the steering angle that would correct the car back to centered within two seconds. Loss: ordinary mean-squared error between predicted and target `1/r`.

**Results.** Evaluated with a custom "autonomy" metric counting simulated human interventions (triggered whenever off-center error exceeds 1 meter) at an assumed cost of 6 seconds each: `autonomy = (1 − interventions·6s / elapsed_time) × 100`. On a simulated three-hour, 100-mile test-route ensemble in Monmouth County, NJ, and on live on-road tests, the paper reports being "autonomous approximately 98% of the time" on a typical office-to-town drive, and completing a 10-mile stretch of the Garden State Parkway "with zero intercepts."

**How it connects.** PilotNet is ALVINN's direct architectural descendant: the same camera-in/steering-out regression framing, the same MSE loss, the same core "let the data pick the features" philosophy — the differences are scale (a real car on real roads, not a simulated corridor) and depth (a five-layer CNN instead of a single hidden layer). Immediately below, Giusti et al. keep the same "camera in, steering-relevant signal out" problem but recast it as classification rather than regression — a genuinely different way to frame the identical BC problem, worth holding side by side with PilotNet's regression choice.

### Paper Breakdown: Visual Perception of Forest Trails (Giusti et al.)

*Citation: Alessandro Giusti, Jérôme Guzzi, Dan C. Cireşan, Fang-Lin He, Juan P. Rodríguez, Flavio Fontana, Matthias Faessler, Christian Forster, Jürgen Schmidhuber, Gianni Di Caro, Davide Scaramuzza, Luca M. Gambardella, "A Machine Learning Approach to Visual Perception of Forest Trails for Mobile Robots," IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, 2016.*

**Problem/motivation.** Following forest trails is far harder to hand-engineer than following a paved road: trails vary enormously in surface, width, and how sharply they stand out from surrounding terrain, so prior approaches based on hand-designed low-level visual cues (appearance contrast, saliency) generalized poorly across trails.

**Key idea/innovation.** Instead of predicting a continuous steering value the way ALVINN/PilotNet do, reframe "which way does the trail go" as a **3-way classification** problem — turn left / go straight / turn right — solved by a CNN trained as an ordinary image classifier.

**Method & training procedure.** Labels are defined by the signed angle α between the camera's own viewing direction and the trail's actual heading: go-straight if |α| is below a 15° threshold β, turn-left/turn-right otherwise, according to which side the trail heading falls on. Training data came from **three head-mounted GoPro cameras** worn by a hiker — one pointing straight ahead (auto-labeled "go straight"), one angled left, one angled right — walking about 7 km of trails over 8 hours of footage, split into 17,119 training and 7,355 held-out test frames with no trail segment shared between the two. The CNN itself is a fairly small, purely convolutional/max-pooling stack (four convolution-plus-max-pooling blocks followed by one fully-connected layer) ending in a 3-neuron softmax output — one probability per class — trained with an ordinary cross-entropy classification loss via backpropagation.

**Results.** 85.2% three-way classification accuracy — close to, and actually falling *between*, two independent human observers scored on the same held-out images (86.5% and 82.0%) — the paper's own conclusion is that "DNN methods perform comparably to humans" on this task. On a simplified two-class version (go-straight vs. not), accuracy rose to 95.0%. A reactive controller built directly from the three class probabilities (steering ∝ P(turn-right) − P(turn-left)) flew a quadrotor along real trail sections for "a few hundred meters" in preliminary tests, though the paper is explicit these flight results are preliminary and reports no exact success rate.

**How it connects.** Giusti et al. is a direct sibling of PilotNet under the exact same recipe named in §2.2 — camera in, steering-relevant signal out, fit by backpropagation, no reward, no expert queried live — but demonstrates that the "steering-relevant signal" doesn't have to be a continuous regression target: casting it as a small number of discrete directional classes works just as well here, and sidesteps needing an exact continuous steering-angle label for every training frame (only a coarse left/straight/right bucket, easy to derive from which of the three cameras a frame came from). Together, PilotNet, Giusti et al., and the classical, non-learned teach-and-repeat system in §2.1 span the real range of ways "get a robot to follow a path it was shown" has actually been attacked in the literature — from no learning at all, to regression, to classification.

## 3. Why imitation learning isn't just supervised learning

### 3.1 Three failure modes, informally

Go back to the driving-instructor analogy from §0. Three distinct things can go wrong:

1. **Covariate shift.** The learner's *own* actions determine which states it visits next. The moment its policy is even slightly different from the expert's, it starts drifting into observations the expert's demonstrations never covered — and because the training data never covered them, the policy has no real signal for what to do there, so it's likely to err *again*, drifting further still. "Covariate" here just means the input distribution (the "X" side of the input→output mapping); the term names the fact that this input distribution *shifts* between training (expert's states) and test (learner's own states) — quietly breaking the standard supervised-learning assumption that train and test data come from the same distribution.
2. **Narrow state-space coverage from cautious demonstrators.** This is a distinct problem from covariate shift, and it exists even before the learner ever acts: a skilled, careful expert's demonstrations are never a random sample of every situation the robot might encounter, because a good demonstrator systematically *avoids* risky, borderline, or failure-adjacent states — a good driver doesn't intentionally drift toward the edge of the lane just to show what "recovering from the edge" looks like. So the demonstrated state distribution is, from the very first frame of data collection, already concentrated on a narrow "things are going well" slice of the full state space. This makes problem 1 above worse rather than being separate from it in effect: because training data barely touches states near failure, the recoverability constant **μ** (§3.2) tends to be largest exactly where a slightly-imperfect learned policy is most likely to actually end up. It's also part of why data augmentation (synthetically manufacturing off-center or off-course examples, as ALVINN's own follow-up work and PilotNet's left/right-camera relabeling both do — see §2.2 and §2.3) is necessary at all: it's compensating for demonstrations that, by construction, almost never show what "recovering" looks like.
3. **[Catastrophic forgetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catastrophic_interference).** If a network is trained incrementally (e.g. on a live stream of new data, the way ALVINN's later on-the-fly version was), a long stretch of one kind of data (e.g. straight highway) can overwrite what the network previously learned about a different kind of data (e.g. sharp curves) it isn't currently seeing — a general pathology of online/streaming neural-network training, not unique to imitation learning, but one BC runs into constantly because demonstrations are naturally collected in whatever order the demonstrator happens to encounter situations.

### 3.2 Formalizing covariate shift: why the error compounds *quadratically*

This is the single most important quantitative idea in Week 1, and it's worth building up carefully, because "the error compounds" undersells how bad the effect actually is.

Picture a robot policy that is trained to match an expert with **per-step error rate ε** — meaning, on any single state drawn from the expert's own demonstrated state distribution, the learned policy disagrees with the expert with probability ε (or, more generally, incurs expected loss ε under some bounded loss function). Naive intuition, borrowed from ordinary supervised learning, says: over a **horizon of T steps**, you'd expect roughly `T · ε` total mistakes — errors are independent per step, so they should just add up.

That intuition is wrong, and the reason is exactly covariate shift. The first time the policy makes a mistake, it lands in a state that likely wasn't in the expert's demonstrated distribution — and the ε guarantee **only ever applied to states the expert actually demonstrated**. In this new, off-distribution state, the policy's error probability isn't bounded by ε at all; it can be much higher, since the policy never trained on anything like it. That elevated error rate then compounds again at the *next* step, and so on.

Formally (Ross & Bagnell 2010's result, restated precisely in this week's DAgger and "Invitation to Imitation" papers): if a policy trained via ordinary BC has expected 0-1 loss ε under the expert's own state distribution, the best *general* guarantee on total task cost is

```
J(policy) ≤ J(expert) + T² · ε
```

— quadratic in the horizon T, not linear. And this bound is **tight**: there exist simple constructed examples (a short Markov chain is enough) where a BC policy really does rack up close to `T²·ε` extra cost, so this isn't a pessimistic worst case that never happens in practice — it's the honest rate.

> **Worked intuition for why T² appears, not just T.** Suppose a mistake, once made, typically takes roughly T more steps to fully "recover" from (imagine drifting off a marked path — getting back on track and finishing the remaining route both take time proportional to how much task remains). If a mistake can happen at any of the T steps, and each one costs (up to) T steps' worth of downstream disruption, the total cost scales like (number of steps) × (cost per mistake) ≈ T × T = T². This is a loose sketch, not the formal proof (which uses a careful induction over per-step distribution shift), but it captures why the horizon appears *twice*: once for "how many chances there are to go wrong," and once for "how long each wrong turn costs you."

Two constants sharpen this picture across the readings this week:

- **u (recovery cost / "how bad is one bad action"):** a bound on how much worse the expert's own future cost-to-go gets after one deviation from its own advice. If the task is very *forgiving* — a wrong turn is easy to correct — u is small (close to 1) and the effective penalty is milder. If a single wrong action can be catastrophic and unrecoverable (u close to T), the T² effect is essentially unavoidable.
- **μ ("recoverability"), the version DAgger's own theory formalizes precisely:** the largest possible gap, at any state, between the value of the expert's chosen action and the value of the worst alternative action, under the expert's own value function. Small μ means "even the expert's worst-looking alternative isn't disastrous" (highly recoverable); large μ means some mistakes are unrecoverable.

### Paper Breakdown: "An Invitation to Imitation" — the framing paper for the whole field

*Citation: J. Andrew Bagnell, "An Invitation to Imitation," CMU Robotics Institute Technical Report CMU-RI-TR-15-08, 2015.*

**Problem/motivation.** This is a survey/tutorial, not a paper reporting new experiments — Bagnell's own decade of lessons on why robotics needs behaviors (driving, legged locomotion, UAV flight) that are easy for a human to *demonstrate* but hard to hand-code, and why naively applying ordinary supervised learning to "programming by demonstration" fails in practice even with abundant, low-error training data. He identifies two specific ways imitation learning breaks the assumptions of classical supervised learning: (1) the learner's predictions change its own future inputs (violating the i.i.d. assumption underlying supervised-learning theory — exactly §3.2's covariate shift, formalized here as the source of the paper's own O(T²ε) result), and (2) real robots are usually built on top of *planners* that reason many steps ahead using a hand-designed cost function, and a purely reactive learned policy that ignores this planning structure performs poorly on long-horizon tasks.

**Key idea/innovation.** Bagnell's unifying reframing: imitation learning problems split into two largely independent families of fixes, each a principled reduction to well-understood machinery. (a) **Interactive reductions to supervised learning** (DAgger, covered in §4) fix covariate shift by letting the learner query the expert along states *it itself* visits, not only the expert's own demonstrated states. (b) **Inverse Optimal Control (IOC)** fixes the "reactive policies can't plan" problem by learning a *cost function* from demonstrations instead of a policy directly, so an existing planner can use it — because cost functions, Bagnell argues, generalize better across situations than directly-learned policies or value functions do.

**Method & training procedure.** The paper's most load-bearing formal content, for this week's purposes, is the precise error-compounding argument in §3.2 above: a BC policy with per-step error ε (measured under the *expert's own* state distribution) has worst-case total cost `J(π) ≤ J(π*) + T²ε`, provably tight. It also describes a conceptually simple (if impractical) exact fix: **forward training** — train a *separate* policy for each timestep t = 1, …, T, always training policy t on the distribution actually induced by running the previously-learned policies for steps 1, …, t−1. Because each timestep's policy is only ever evaluated on the exact distribution it was trained on, no covariate shift ever occurs, and the cost bound improves to `J(π) ≤ J(π*) + u·T·ε` (linear in T, using the "u" recovery-cost constant from §3.2) — but training T separate policies is impractical whenever the horizon is long or variable. On the IOC side, the paper walks through **LEARCH** (Learning to Search), a functional-gradient/boosting algorithm for learning a cost map from demonstrated paths: repeatedly (a) plan a route under the current cost estimate, (b) compare that route to the demonstrated one state-by-state (raising the learned cost anywhere the plan went that the demonstration avoided, lowering it anywhere the demonstration went that the plan avoided), (c) fit a regressor to these adjustments, and (d) add it to the running cost estimate — provably a form of gradient boosting that converges over several iterations, not in one shot.

**Results.** As a survey paper it reports no controlled experiments of its own, only illustrative case studies from Bagnell's and collaborators' prior work: a driving-simulator comparison where plain supervised BC "averages about 3–4 failures per lap" while an interactive (DAgger-style) policy "very quickly reaches nearly 0 falls per lap" no matter how much more data plain BC gets; a UAV reactive forest-flight controller performing "at nearly the same effectiveness as a human pilot," with failures traced to its limited field of view rather than the learning method; and a DARPA-funded rough-terrain ground vehicle ("Crusher") that, using a learned LEARCH-style cost function, traversed "thousands of kilometers of diverse, rough terrain with minimal human intervention over years of field testing."

**How it connects.** This paper is the conceptual umbrella over almost the entire lecture: its O(T²ε) result is the formal justification for why §4's DAgger exists at all, and its "learn a cost, not a policy" IOC framing is the direct ancestor of the inverse-reinforcement-learning unit in Week 6. Its forward-training construction is also the conceptual bridge to DAgger's own theory in §4 — DAgger can be understood as forward training's practical, single-policy approximation.

### 3.3 Catastrophic forgetting and the online-learning buffer fix

Separately from covariate shift, a policy trained incrementally on a live data stream (rather than one fixed batch) can simply **forget** — new data overwrites what old data taught it, especially if the environment or task distribution "drifts" over time (a phenomenon sometimes called **[concept drift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept_drift)**: the statistical relationship between inputs and correct outputs itself changes over time, e.g. lighting conditions, road types, or object appearances shifting as a demonstration session goes on). This is a general pathology of online learning and reinforcement learning, not unique to imitation learning — but BC hits it often because demonstrations naturally arrive in whatever order the demonstrator happens to encounter situations, not shuffled i.i.d. the way a static supervised-learning dataset would be.

The standard mitigation, already previewed in §2's ALVINN discussion, is an **experience replay buffer**: instead of training only on the newest incoming data, maintain a fixed-size buffer of past (observation, action) examples, and train on a mix of new and buffered-old examples every update — actively curated (in ALVINN's later version, by biasing the buffer's contents to keep the *average* recorded action close to some neutral baseline) so that rare-but-important situations (like sharp turns, which occur less often than straight driving) don't get diluted away by whatever's most recent.

## 4. DAgger: fixing covariate shift by querying the expert on your own mistakes

### 4.1 The core idea

If covariate shift happens because the training data only covers the *expert's* states, the direct fix is: let the *learner's own* policy generate states, and get expert labels for *those* states too. That's the entirety of DAgger's ("Dataset Aggregation") idea — an iterative loop:

1. Train an initial policy on whatever demonstration data you already have.
2. Roll that policy out in the real environment (let it drive, or manipulate, on its own).
3. At every state the policy visits during that rollout, ask the expert: "what would *you* have done here?" — the expert's answer is recorded as a label, but the expert's own answer is **not** what actually gets executed; the learner's own (possibly wrong) action keeps driving the rollout forward, precisely so that later states in the rollout are the ones the learner's mistakes would actually lead to.
4. Add all these newly-labeled (learner-visited-state, expert-action) pairs into the training set (which keeps growing — this is the "aggregation" in the name).
5. Retrain a fresh policy on the whole aggregated dataset so far.
6. Repeat.

Over enough iterations, the aggregated dataset increasingly covers the states the *learner itself* tends to visit, not just the states the expert originally happened to visit — directly attacking the covariate-shift mechanism from §3.2.

### 4.2 No-regret online learning — the theoretical lens DAgger is built on

DAgger's guarantees are proven by treating each iteration of the loop as one round of an **[online learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_machine_learning)** problem, and requiring the supervised-learning step (step 5 above) to behave like a **no-regret** algorithm.

**[Regret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regret_(decision_theory))**, informally: how much worse a sequence of decisions performs, in total, compared to the single best *fixed* decision you could have made in hindsight, knowing everything in advance. A learner has **no regret** if this gap, averaged over N rounds, shrinks to zero as N grows — i.e. it eventually performs about as well as the best fixed choice it could only have known in hindsight, even though it had to commit to each round's choice before seeing what was coming.

```
regret_N = (1/N) · Σ loss_i(policy_i) − min over π of (1/N) · Σ loss_i(π)
```

A no-regret guarantee needs `regret_N → 0` as N → ∞. Many practical algorithms (e.g. training via gradient descent when the loss is strongly convex) provably have this property.

### 4.3 Why this fixes the quadratic bound

DAgger's central theoretical payoff: **if the retraining step in the loop is (or behaves like) a no-regret online learner**, then somewhere among the sequence of policies it produces — or a policy chosen uniformly at random from that sequence — there is one whose total task cost is bounded by

```
J(policy) ≤ J(expert) + u · T · ε_N + O(1)
```

**linear** in the horizon T (using the same recovery-cost constant u from §3.2), where ε_N is the average loss of the *best policy in hindsight* over all the iterations' aggregated data. This is the direct improvement over plain BC's `T² · ε` bound from §3.2 — the same style of guarantee forward training achieved in §3's "Invitation to Imitation" discussion, but without needing to train a separate policy per timestep.

### Paper Breakdown: DAgger

*Citation: Stéphane Ross, Geoffrey J. Gordon, J. Andrew Bagnell, "A Reduction of Imitation Learning and Structured Prediction to No-Regret Online Learning," AISTATS 2011.*

**Problem/motivation.** Formalizes exactly the covariate-shift diagnosis from §3.2 and proves it's tight (via a sequence-prediction construction where mistakes provably compound at close to the T² rate), then asks: can an *iterative*, interactive training procedure recover a linear-in-T guarantee, the way forward training does, without forward training's impracticality (a separate policy per timestep)?

**Key idea/innovation.** The DAgger loop from §4.1, reframed as a **reduction of imitation learning to no-regret online learning**: treat each iteration's retraining step as one round of an online-learning problem, and show that *any* no-regret algorithm used this way — not some special-purpose imitation-learning trick — yields the linear-in-T guarantee.

**Method & training procedure.** The exact algorithm, as given in the paper (β_i is the probability of querying the expert, rather than the learner's own current policy, at iteration i's rollout — used only to collect data, never to decide what the aggregated dataset's *labels* are):

```
DAgger(π̂_1, expert, N):
    D ← ∅
    for i = 1 to N:
        π_i ← β_i · expert + (1 − β_i) · π̂_i      # mixture policy used only to ROLL OUT
        roll out π_i for T steps, collecting visited states
        D_i ← {(state, expert(state)) : state visited above}   # expert LABELS every visited state
        D ← D ∪ D_i                                              # aggregate
        π̂_{i+1} ← train a new policy on all of D
    return best π̂_i on a held-out validation set (or a uniformly random one — both provably work)
```

A common, "parameter-free" special case sets β_1 = 1 (first iteration is pure expert demonstration, since there's no learned policy yet) and β_i = 0 for i > 1 (every later iteration rolls out the learner's own policy alone) — the paper notes this variant "often performs best in practice." The only formal requirement on β_i for the theory to hold is that its running average shrinks to 0 as iterations increase. The paper proves this reduction works for *any* no-regret learner (Theorem 3.1/3.2), and separately proves finite-sample versions (Theorems 3.3/3.4) bounding how many total rollout trajectories (`N`) are needed.

**Results.** Three experiments, each comparing plain supervised BC, the earlier SMILe/SEARN-style methods, and DAgger:
- **SuperTuxKart** (steering a kart via a linear ridge-regression controller on image features): plain BC's performance never improves no matter how much more expert-only data it gets (the falls happen off-distribution); SMILe still falls roughly twice per lap after 20 iterations; **DAgger reaches essentially zero falls per lap after 15 iterations**, and is already close after just 5.
- **Super Mario Bros.** (4 independent linear SVMs for left/right/jump/speed, scored by distance traveled before dying): plain BC stagnates because it never learns to "get unstuck" from obstacles (the expert always avoided getting near one). DAgger **outperforms every tested SMILe and SEARN configuration**; best variant reaches a score of 3030 out of a max around 4300 on the test stage.
- **Handwriting recognition**, reframed as a degenerate imitation-learning problem (predicting each character left-to-right, conditioned on the *previously predicted* — not ground-truth — character, exactly mirroring how a policy's own past actions shape its future inputs): a non-sequential baseline gets 82% character accuracy; standard "supervised" training (always conditioning on the *true* previous character, never the model's own guess) gets 83.6%; **DAgger reaches 85.5%**, the best of all methods tested.

**How it connects.** DAgger is the direct, practical instantiation of the "Invitation to Imitation" paper's forward-training idea, replacing "one policy per timestep" with "one continuously-retrained policy, trained on an ever-growing, learner-informed dataset." Its core limitation — needing to query a live expert repeatedly, which is often impossible outside a simulator or a very patient human — is precisely the gap the next section's paper addresses from a completely different angle.

### 4.4 DAgger leaves the simulator: reactive UAV flight through a forest

DAgger's own paper (§4.3 above) validates its linear-vs-quadratic guarantee only on simulated kart racing, a video game, and handwriting recognition — none of them a physical robot with real inertia, real sensor noise, and real consequences for a wrong turn. The same lead authors' next paper closes exactly that gap.

### Paper Breakdown: Learning Monocular Reactive UAV Control in Cluttered Natural Environments

*Citation: Stéphane Ross, Narek Melik-Barkhudarov, Kumar Shaurya Shankar, Andreas Wendel, Debadeepta Dey, J. Andrew Bagnell, Martial Hebert, "Learning Monocular Reactive UAV Control in Cluttered Natural Environments," ICRA 2013.*

**Problem/motivation.** Small flying robots ("micro aerial vehicles") can only carry very light sensors — often just a single camera — so dodging obstacles like trees at any real flying speed leaves no time for heavyweight 3D mapping or long-horizon planning. The paper frames its own contribution directly against the original DAgger paper: "[DAgger] demonstrated successful application of this technique in simulated environments (video game applications); our experiments show that this technique can also be successfully applied on real robotic platforms."

**Key idea/innovation.** Train a purely **reactive** controller — no map, no planner, just "camera image in, one steering command out," built on hand-designed visual features rather than a full CNN — for a small quadrotor to dodge trees at flying speed, using the DAgger loop from §4.1 with a real human pilot standing in as the expert.

**Method & training procedure.** Platform: a 420-gram Parrot AR.Drone (an AR.Drone 2.0 with an HD camera for the densest test), flown with forward speed and altitude held fixed, so the only learned decision is left/right yaw. Input features are hand-designed, not learned end-to-end the way ALVINN/PilotNet's CNNs are: statistics from a Radon transform, a structure tensor, texture ("Laws' masks"), and optical flow, computed over a grid of overlapping windows across the camera frame, plus a few onboard sensor readings (drift, heading deviation, recent command history) — fed into a single linear controller trained by ridge regression, not a neural network. Data collection followed the DAgger loop from §4.1 almost exactly, with one necessary real-world safety modification: because the drone is a physical object that can actually crash into a tree, the human pilot doesn't just passively label states after the fact — they watch the drone fly under its own current policy (sometimes via a slowed-down, 3×-decelerated video replay, since real-time reaction wasn't always fast enough) and either log a corrective joystick command without it being executed (ordinary DAgger labeling) or physically take over/force an emergency landing when a real crash is imminent. The paper notes this restricts the collected data to a "safe" subset of what pure DAgger would visit, but argues DAgger's guarantees still hold as long as a policy that stays inside that safe region is learnable at all.

**Results.** Trained over 3 DAgger iterations in each setting. In a sparse outdoor park (about 1 tree per 12×12 m), the drone flew at roughly 1 m/s and, after training, generalized to a held-out test area, flying autonomously "over a 100 m distance" without crashing before reaching the limit of the paper's communication range. In dense natural forest (about 1 tree per 3×3 m — thick enough to need dodging roughly once every 5 meters), the same recipe flew at up to 1.5 m/s and the paper reports the drone "successfully avoided more than 680 trees during flights over a distance of more than 3 km" across all experiments combined. The paper reports no numeric head-to-head comparison against a plain, non-interactive BC baseline on this task — the case for DAgger over BC here rests on the theoretical compounding-error argument of §3.2, not a side-by-side robot flight.

**How it connects.** This paper is the physical-robot proof that DAgger's interactive-query loop (§4.1) survives contact with real sensor noise, real flight dynamics, and a real human who can't always react instantly — exactly the validation the original DAgger paper's simulated/game benchmarks (§4.3) couldn't provide on their own. Its "safe subset of DAgger" compromise — querying the pilot but restricting what states actually get flown into — is also an early, informal ancestor of the more principled question §8 asks next: exactly when is it necessary to query the expert at all, rather than trusting the learner's current policy?

## 5. A second fix: changing the loss function instead of querying the expert live

DAgger fixes covariate shift, but at a real cost: it needs the expert available *during training*, repeatedly, to label states the (partially-trained) policy visits. For a human demonstrator teleoperating a physical robot, or for many real deployments, this is expensive or simply infeasible — you usually only get to collect demonstrations once, offline, and then train.

This raises a natural question, and one that unsettled some of the field's long-standing "folk wisdom": is the T² vs. T gap between offline BC and online/interactive methods like DAgger a fundamental property of *offline learning itself* — or is it partly an artifact of exactly *how* BC's classical analysis measures error, and of exactly *how much* it insists on querying the expert?

Two papers this section pairs answer that question from genuinely different directions, and are worth reading back to back specifically because they disagree about where the fix belongs. The first, immediately below, proposes a new *training algorithm*: never query a live expert unconditionally the way DAgger does — instead, roll out the learner's own current policy, measure statistically how far its own visited states have drifted from the expert's demonstrated ones, and use that measurement to decide, per state, whether an expert query is even worth asking for. The second, further below, changes no algorithm at all — it re-examines plain, offline BC's own classical error analysis, and shows that the extra factor of horizon everyone assumes offline BC is stuck with is partly an artifact of *how error is measured*, not an unavoidable property of training without live queries. One paper is an algorithm; the other is a sharper analysis of an already-standard algorithm — two different kinds of answer to the identical question this section just posed.

### Paper Breakdown: Maximum Mean Discrepancy Imitation Learning (MMD-IL)

*Citation: Beomjoon Kim, Joelle Pineau, "Maximum Mean Discrepancy Imitation Learning," Robotics: Science and Systems (RSS), 2013.*

**Problem/motivation.** DAgger (§4) has two practical drawbacks the authors identify directly. First, DAgger's own theory only ever lets the expert *label* the learner's visited states — the expert is never allowed to actually take over control, even when an early, poorly-trained policy is heading somewhere genuinely dangerous, which the paper flags as a real safety concern "particularly problematic in the early phases of training." Second, DAgger's aggregation strategy queries the expert at *every* visited state and treats every query as equally valuable, which can demand large amounts of expensive expert time (the paper's own example: helicopter piloting) just to recover from an early policy's mistakes.

**Key idea/innovation.** Rather than querying the expert at every single visited state and always executing the learner's own (possibly bad) action the way plain DAgger does, MMD-IL trains a small family of policies — one per iteration — and, at each new state, uses a purely statistical measurement of "how similar is this situation to what I've already trained on" to decide, on the fly: (a) which of the already-trained policies to actually run, and (b) whether the current situation is unfamiliar enough to be worth an expert query at all, and if it is queried, whether the expert's answer disagreed enough with what the policy would have done to be worth adding to a growing training set.

**Building Maximum Mean Discrepancy from scratch.** The tool making decisions (a) and (b) possible is the **[Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_embedding_of_distributions)**, a way to measure how different two collections of samples are *without ever needing either collection's actual probability density function* — which matters here because nobody has a formula for "the density of states the expert's demonstrations visited," only a finite pile of recorded example states.

> Picture two boxes of photographs — one from your own last vacation, one from a random stranger's. Without knowing anything about how photos are formally distributed, you could still get a decent read on "were these two vacations similar?" by picking a few useful summary statistics (average brightness, how outdoorsy the scenes look, how many photos have people in them) and comparing each statistic's *average* between the two boxes. If every summary tried comes out about the same for both boxes, the vacations were probably genuinely alike; if even one well-chosen summary comes out very different, they weren't. MMD formalizes "well-chosen summary" by considering every function in a suitably rich function class at once, and reporting the single *worst-case* gap: `MMD = sup` over functions `f` in that class of `(average of f over box 1) − (average of f over box 2)`. Because the function class used (built from a chosen kernel, forming what's called a [reproducing kernel Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproducing_kernel_Hilbert_space)) is rich enough, a clean guarantee holds: this worst-case gap is exactly zero if and only if the two underlying distributions are actually identical — so MMD is a genuine distance between distributions, computable directly from finite samples via the kernel, with no density function required anywhere. (This plays a role comparable to the squared Hellinger distance used in the Log-loss BC paper covered next in this section — both measure "how far apart are two distributions," just for different purposes: Hellinger distance there measures how far a *learned policy's whole trajectory distribution* sits from the *expert's*, inside a theoretical regret bound; MMD here measures, at run time, how far a *single current state* sits from a *dataset of previously-seen states*, used to make an operational decision.)

**Method & training procedure.** Concretely (their Algorithm 1): maintain a growing sequence of datasets `D_0, D_1, ..., D_i` (D_0 seeded from initial expert demonstrations) and one policy trained on each, `π̂_0, ..., π̂_i` — each trained by ordinary supervised regression, exactly like a single BC/DAgger training step, not by gradient-descending on MMD directly. At every newly-visited state `s`, compute `MMD[s, D_j]` (a closed-form kernel calculation, no search or training involved) against every dataset seen so far, and run whichever policy was trained on the *closest* dataset. Only if that minimum MMD exceeds a threshold `α` (meaning: this state looks unlike anything trained on yet) does the system query the expert at all — and even then, the expert's answer is only added to the next training dataset if it disagrees with what the selected policy would have done by more than a second threshold `γ` (meaning: don't bother recording agreement, only genuine mistakes). The two thresholds make the safety/efficiency trade-off explicit: `α = 0` queries the expert at every step (maximal safety, closest to plain DAgger); a large `α` queries rarely (maximal savings on expert time). The paper proves a regret bound of the same *shape* as DAgger's own (§4.3) — loss growing linearly, not quadratically, in the horizon — under an assumption bounding how much any single expert intervention can shift the state distribution.

**Results.** On a simulated car brake-control task, "MMD-IL is shown to perform much better than DAgger even using only around 200 oracle demonstration actions (one iteration), whereas DAgger needed at least 800 data points (eight iterations) to achieve near competitive performance." On a real wheelchair-robot navigation task (using an RGB-D camera to sense a 3×7 grid of nearby obstacle density and motion), MMD-IL "learns to navigate by itself after iteration 3, compared to iteration 7 for DAgger" in a static-obstacle environment, and produced trajectories that "imitated the oracle almost perfectly" around a moving pedestrian in a dynamic environment, versus a final DAgger policy the paper describes as "less efficient" and "socially ineffective" (it stopped and waited rather than navigating around the pedestrian).

**How it connects.** MMD-IL is the algorithmic answer to this section's opening question — a genuinely different *training procedure* from plain DAgger, built around measuring distributional novelty rather than querying unconditionally. Its "query only when the current state looks unfamiliar" design is also a direct conceptual ancestor of the uncertainty-driven querying methods in §8, though MMD-IL's notion of "unfamiliar" is a distributional-distance calculation over recorded states, rather than any of §8's model-uncertainty estimates.

### Paper Breakdown: "Is Behavior Cloning All You Need? Understanding Horizon in Imitation Learning"

*Citation: Dylan J. Foster, Adam Block, Dipendra Misra, arXiv:2407.15007, 2024 (accepted to NeurIPS 2024).*

**Problem/motivation.** The classical result from §3–§4 (BC: `T²·ε`; DAgger-style online IL: `T·ε` under a recoverability condition) is the textbook justification for preferring interactive methods over plain offline BC. But interactive querying is often infeasible in practice, and offline BC remains the dominant method used empirically despite its supposedly worse theoretical guarantee. The authors ask directly: is offline BC truly stuck with quadratic sample complexity, or can a better *analysis* — not a different algorithm — close the gap?

**Key idea/innovation.** The classical `T²·ε` bound is derived using **0-1 (indicator) loss** — did the policy's action exactly match the expert's, yes or no — and measures the resulting distribution mismatch in **total variation distance**. The paper shows that if you instead train BC with the **log loss** (maximum-likelihood / cross-entropy over the predicted action distribution — the same objective used ubiquitously in practice, e.g. next-token prediction in language models) and measure the resulting error in **squared [Hellinger distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellinger_distance)** between whole predicted-vs-expert *trajectory* distributions (rather than distance at a single state), the extra factor of T that plain BC's analysis picks up when translating "per-state prediction error" into "total rollout cost" simply disappears from the bound.

**Method & training procedure.** Precisely: with `LogLossBC` defined as maximum-likelihood training of the policy against the expert's action distribution,

```
LogLossBC:  π̂ = argmin over π in Π of  Σ_i Σ_h  −log( π_h(a_h^i | x_h^i) )
```

the paper proves a regret bound of the shape (their Theorem 2.1, for deterministic experts):

```
J(expert) − J(π̂) ≤ 4·R · D²_Hellinger(trajectory distribution of π̂, trajectory distribution of expert)
```

where R bounds the *total* cumulative reward range across an episode (R ≤ H for ordinary per-step-bounded rewards, but R = O(1) for many sparse-reward tasks) — critically, this bound scales with R, **not with R·H**, i.e. no extra horizon factor is multiplying the supervised-learning error term at all. Combined with a standard maximum-likelihood generalization bound, this yields **horizon-independent** sample complexity whenever two conditions hold: (1) the cumulative reward range R is controlled (doesn't grow with H — true for sparse-reward or otherwise bounded-payoff tasks), and (2) the policy class has controlled *supervised-learning* complexity, which in practice essentially means **parameter sharing across timesteps** — a single network (a transformer, an MLP) applied identically at every decision step, as is standard in essentially all modern IL, rather than a distinct policy per timestep. Under **dense** per-step rewards (R = O(H)) the bound degrades gracefully to *linear*-in-H — still an improvement over the classical quadratic rate, and matching what DAgger achieves, but without ever querying an expert live.

The paper is explicit about why this doesn't contradict DAgger's own theory: DAgger's advantage over plain BC, in the *classical* analysis, only shows up for policy classes **without** parameter sharing (a distinct policy per timestep, the setting the older tabular analyses implicitly assumed) — and "virtually all empirical [imitation learning] work uses parameter sharing." For a shared-parameter policy class, their reanalysis shows plain offline log-loss BC already matches what online DAgger-style methods buy you, in the regimes that matter in practice.

**Results.** Across four testbeds — a MuJoCo continuous-control task (Walker2d), an Atari game (Beam Rider), a custom sparse-reward top-down navigation task, and training a small GPT-2-style transformer to generate valid bracket sequences — the empirically measured regret of LogLossBC is essentially **flat as the horizon H grows** on the tasks where their theory predicts flatness (sparse reward, parameter sharing), confirming the theoretical prediction directly; on the one task (bracket-sequence generation) where regret *does* grow with H, they show this tracks a genuine growth in how complex a policy is needed to represent the expert at longer horizons, not a violation of the theory. A companion finding: log-loss BC is also markedly more *robust* to imperfect optimization than 0-1-loss BC — under a fixed amount of optimization error, indicator-loss BC's bound degrades by a full extra factor of H, while log-loss BC's degrades by no horizon factor at all.

**How it connects.** This paper doesn't overturn DAgger's guarantee so much as clarify exactly *when* it actually buys you something over well-analyzed offline BC — for the parameter-shared neural network policies used throughout the rest of this course (including nearly every architecture in §6–§7 below), the honest headline is closer to "the loss function and reward structure you choose matter as much as whether you query the expert live," rather than "offline BC is fundamentally doomed to quadratic error." Read together with MMD-IL just above, the two papers land on the same lesson from two different directions: MMD-IL shows that changing *what triggers a query* can already buy back DAgger's linear rate without querying at every visited state, while this paper shows that even querying *never* can buy back the same linear rate, provided the loss function and reward structure are chosen well.

## 6. Teleoperation: how demonstrations for manipulation actually get collected

Sections 3–5 treated "getting expert-labeled data" as an abstraction. For robot *manipulation* specifically (rather than driving, where a human sitting in the vehicle already naturally provides steering/pedal data), that data almost always comes from **teleoperation** — a human, in real time, driving a robot's actual arm(s) and hand(s) through the task, while the robot logs its own sensor readings and the resulting commands. The five papers in this section are all, at heart, about *better teleoperation hardware/interfaces* — because the quality, diversity, and cost of collectible demonstrations bottlenecks everything built on top of them.

### 6.1 A shared building block: action chunking

Before the individual systems, one recurring design idea is worth introducing on its own, since four of this week's papers use some version of it. A plain BC policy predicts **one action at a time**: observe, act, observe again, act again. **Action chunking** instead has the policy predict a short **sequence** ("chunk") of several future actions in a single forward pass, of which typically only the first few are actually executed before the robot re-observes and re-plans (a pattern control theory calls **[receding-horizon control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_predictive_control)** — plan further ahead than you commit to, and keep re-planning as new information arrives).

Why this helps, connecting back to §3.2: querying the policy less often (because each query commits to several steps at once) shortens the *effective* decision horizon, which directly shrinks the T²-type compounding-error penalty. It also helps with a second, more practical problem: real human teleoperation demonstrations are noisy and **non-stationary** — pauses, hesitations, small corrections — and a policy predicting only the very next action can get confused about whether "pause" or "continue" is correct at a given instant, in a way that predicting a whole short future sequence is more robust to.

The two architectures among this week's readings that actually implement chunking — ACT and Diffusion Policy — are taught together, in full architectural depth, in §7.4, since both solve the same "predict a chunk of future actions" problem with different generative mechanisms.

### 6.2 Leader-follower teleoperation and Action Chunking Transformers

The most direct way to teleoperate a bimanual robot is a **leader-follower** rig: a scaled-down, cheap, *passive* (unpowered) mechanical duplicate of the robot's own arm that a human physically holds and moves, with its joint sensors' readings sent, joint-by-joint, as position commands to the real ("follower") robot arm.

### Paper Breakdown: ALOHA

*Citation: Tony Z. Zhao, Vikash Kumar, Sergey Levine, Chelsea Finn, "Learning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware," RSS 2023.*

**Problem/motivation.** Fine-grained bimanual manipulation (threading a cable tie, slotting a battery into a holder) traditionally needed expensive, precisely-calibrated industrial hardware — putting this kind of research out of reach for most labs. Separately, BC in these high-precision settings suffers from both compounding error (§3.2) and the non-stationarity problem noted in §6.1 (human demonstrations include pauses/hesitations a naive single-step policy struggles to fit).

**Key idea/innovation.** Pair a **cheap ($20k total), open-source leader-follower bimanual teleoperation rig** with a new policy architecture, the **Action Chunking Transformer (ACT)**, that predicts chunks of future actions via a generative model — together letting inexpensive, imprecise hardware achieve 80–90%+ success on fine manipulation from only 10–20 minutes of demonstration per task.

**Method & training procedure.** Hardware: two off-the-shelf **WidowX-250** arms (leader, human-held) and two **ViperX-300** arms (follower), all built on cheap, easily-replaceable Dynamixel servo motors. Crucially, the leader→follower mapping is done in **joint space**, not task space (§1) — each leader joint angle is sent directly as a position command to the matching follower joint, avoiding inverse-kinematics singularities and keeping control latency low; the system runs at 50 Hz (a user study found dropping to 5 Hz caused a 62% task-completion slowdown). Four wrist- and scene-mounted RGB cameras provide vision. ALOHA's hardware pairs with a new policy architecture, the **Action Chunking Transformer (ACT)**, which predicts a whole chunk of future actions per forward pass through a conditional generative model. ACT's full architecture — its conditional-variational-autoencoder mechanics, its transformer decoder, and its temporal-ensembling inference procedure — is taught in depth in §7.4, alongside Diffusion Policy, since the two architectures solve the same "predict many steps ahead" problem with genuinely different generative mechanisms and are best understood side by side.

**Results.** Six real tasks, 50 demonstrations each (100 for the hardest, "Thread Velcro"): success rates from 20% (Thread Velcro) up to 96% (Slot Battery), with the two showcase tasks (opening a translucent cup, slotting a battery) headlined in the abstract at "80–90%" (the paper's own detailed per-task table reports 84% for the cup and 96% for the battery — the abstract's range is an approximate summary, not each task's literal figure). Ablations isolate the two design choices cleanly: sweeping the chunk size from k=1 to k=100 raised success from 1% to 44% on one task in isolation, and removing the CVAE/generative component specifically hurt performance on *noisy, human-collected* data (35.3% → 2%) while making almost no difference on noise-free scripted data — direct evidence the CVAE is earning its keep on real human demonstration noise specifically, not just generically helpful.

**How it connects.** ACT itself is taught in full in §7.4, together with Diffusion Policy — the two are presented and contrasted side by side there, since both generate action chunks but via different generative mechanisms (single-shot conditional decoding vs. iterative denoising). Separately, the leader-follower rig itself is directly critiqued — for being robot-specific and pushing all safety/collision-avoidance onto the human — by the exoskeleton-based systems in §6.5–§6.6.

**A brief note on Mobile ALOHA (not itself a required reading).** A follow-up system (Fu, Zhao, Finn, CoRL 2024) mounts the same leader-follower arms on a wheeled mobile base, with the human's own waist tethered to the low-friction, back-drivable base so that walking drags it along while both hands stay free for the arms — enabling whole-body tasks like cooking or opening cabinets. Its main empirical finding directly reinforces a theme of this whole lecture: **co-training** new mobile-task demonstrations together with the original static-ALOHA dataset dramatically improved data efficiency, raising success rates by up to 90 percentage points from only 50 new demonstrations per task — reusing old data to make new data go further, rather than needing enormous fresh datasets per task.

### 6.3 Decoupling data collection from any particular robot

Leader-follower rigs, precisely because they're built to match one specific robot's kinematics, only ever collect data *for that robot*, in a lab, with that robot physically present. The next system asks: what if you didn't need a robot at all to collect manipulation data?

### Paper Breakdown: UMI (Universal Manipulation Interface)

*Citation: Cheng Chi, Zhenjia Xu, Chuer Pan, Eric Cousineau, Benjamin Burchfiel, Siyuan Feng, Russ Tedrake, Shuran Song, "Universal Manipulation Interface: In-The-Wild Robot Teaching Without In-The-Wild Robots," RSS 2024.*

**Problem/motivation.** Two compounding bottlenecks: standard demonstration collection (teleoperation, kinesthetic teaching) needs a *physical robot on site*, making it expensive and slow to scale data collection across the diversity of real-world environments (homes, restaurants, outdoors) a general-purpose policy would need to see; and policies trained on one robot's action representation (its specific joint encoders/kinematics) typically don't transfer to a differently-shaped robot without retraining.

**Key idea/innovation.** A **hand-held, GoPro-instrumented gripper** lets a human collect demonstrations anywhere, with no robot present at all; an **embodiment-agnostic action representation** — relative end-effector motion rather than absolute joint angles — then lets the same collected data train policies that deploy zero-shot on different physical robot arms.

**Method & training procedure.** The device: a 3D-printed gripper (~$73 in parts) with a wrist-mounted GoPro (wide fisheye lens) as its *only* sensor — no external motion-capture. Side mirrors placed in the camera's peripheral view create an implicit stereo (depth) cue from a single lens. The gripper's own 6-DOF pose over time is recovered purely from the recorded video via an inertial-monocular **[SLAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_localization_and_mapping)** system (a modified ORB-SLAM3, fusing the GoPro's built-in accelerometer/gyroscope with visual tracking) — average tracking error only ~6.1 mm / 3.5° versus motion-capture ground truth. Actions and observations are represented as **relative SE(3) poses** (this gripper's pose *relative to* a recent reference pose) plus a gripper-width value, rather than any robot's absolute joint angles — a representation any robot's own inverse-kinematics controller can consume regardless of how many joints or what kinematic chain it has, which is precisely what makes the same collected data usable across different arms. A **Diffusion Policy** (§7.4) trained on this representation, conditioned on the wrist camera's video, predicts chunks of future relative poses.

**Results.** Tested zero-shot on both a UR5 arm (its "home" collection platform) and a completely different Franka arm: cup-arrangement success stayed at 100% on the UR5 and 90% on the untrained-for Franka. An "in-the-wild" cup-arrangement dataset (1,400 demonstrations collected across 30 different real-world locations — homes, offices, restaurants — by 3 people in just 12 person-hours) reached 71.7% success overall, including 75% on cup styles never seen during data collection. Other tasks (dynamic tossing, bimanual cloth folding, dish washing) ranged 70–87.5% success.

**How it connects.** UMI's relative-pose, embodiment-agnostic representation is the direct opposite design choice from ALOHA's joint-space leader-follower mapping in §6.2 — a genuine design axis worth noticing across this section: mirror the target robot exactly (ALOHA) versus abstract away from any particular robot entirely (UMI). Its use of Diffusion Policy as the downstream learner is explained in full in §7.4.

### 6.4 VR-based dexterous hand tracking

A different approach to teleoperation skips *any* physical controller rig and instead tracks the human operator's own body directly with a consumer VR/AR headset.

### Paper Breakdown: Bunny-VisionPro

*Citation: Runyu Ding, Yuzhe Qin, Jiyue Zhu, Chengzhe Jia, Shiqi Yang, Ruihan Yang, Xiaojuan Qi, Xiaolong Wang, "Bunny-VisionPro: Real-Time Bimanual Dexterous Teleoperation for Imitation Learning," arXiv:2407.03162, 2024.*

**Problem/motivation.** Existing teleoperation approaches for **dexterous** (multi-fingered, humanlike-hand) bimanual manipulation each have a gap: leader-follower rigs (§6.2) are robot-specific and push all singularity/collision avoidance onto the human operator; earlier vision-based hand-tracking systems needed heavy GPU processing and were mostly single-arm only; and almost none of these systems give the operator any haptic (touch) feedback, or handle the noisy, self-occluded finger tracking that consumer headsets like Apple Vision Pro actually produce.

**Key idea/innovation.** Use a consumer VR headset purely as a markerless hand-pose *sensor*, and solve the human-to-robot motion mapping as a fast, real-time constrained optimization that simultaneously matches the robot's hand shape to the human's, stays smooth frame-to-frame, and automatically respects joint limits/avoids collisions and singularities — while feeding back cheap vibration-motor haptic cues to the operator's fingertips.

**Method & training procedure.** An Apple Vision Pro tracks the operator's two hands (no gloves or markers). Each frame, a constrained least-squares optimization (solved via **[Sequential Quadratic Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequential_quadratic_programming)**) finds robot joint angles that (a) place the robot hand's fingertips near the human's tracked fingertip positions, (b) penalize large frame-to-frame joint changes for smoothness, and (c) respect joint limits — with a reformulation that solves only over the robot hand's actively-driven joints (not its full, larger set of mechanically-coupled joints), reported as a 10× speedup. Hardware: two 7-DOF xArm arms, each fitted with a 6-DOF "Ability Hand." Haptic feedback: one $1.20 vibration motor per fingertip (10 total), worn via fabric fingertip sleeves, driven by a microcontroller reading contact-force sensors on the robot hand. Downstream policies (ACT, Diffusion Policy, and 3D Diffusion Policy) were trained on collected demonstrations for comparison.

**Results.** On a 10-task single-arm benchmark, Bunny-VisionPro matched or beat two prior teleoperation baselines on every task (e.g. 10/10 vs. 9/10 and 10/10 on one task; 9/10 vs. 3/10 and 7/10 on a harder two-cup-stacking task). On custom bimanual tasks it reported an 11% higher success rate with 45% less completion time than a strong baseline. Averaged across the three downstream policy architectures, training on Bunny-VisionPro-collected data gave a 22% success-rate improvement, with the haptic feedback specifically improving or maintaining success in 9 of 10 tested comparisons. The whole teleoperation loop ran above 60 Hz.

**How it connects.** Like ACE (§6.6), this system explicitly targets what it sees as leader-follower teleoperation's weak point (robot-specificity, no built-in safety) using a very different mechanism (markerless vision instead of a passive mechanical rig) — comparing the two is a useful exercise in seeing that "better teleoperation" has more than one defensible engineering answer.

### 6.5 Active, steerable viewpoints

A separate design axis from *how the operator's arms/hands are tracked* is *what the operator sees* while teleoperating — and a fixed camera view turns out to be a real bottleneck of its own.

### Paper Breakdown: Open-TeleVision ("Robot-TV")

*Citation: Xuxin Cheng, Jialong Li, Shiqi Yang, Ge Yang, Xiaolong Wang, "Open-TeleVision: Teleoperation with Immersive Active Visual Feedback," CoRL 2024.*

**Problem/motivation.** A standard fixed third-person camera bolted somewhere in the workspace inevitably gets occluded by the robot's own arms or body at exactly the moments precision matters most, so the operator can't be sure the demonstration actually captured what the downstream policy will need to see — silently degrading collected training data, not just making teleoperation feel harder.

**Key idea/innovation.** Mount a **stereo (two-eyed) camera on a small, actively-steerable robot "head,"** driven in real time by the operator's own VR headset head-tracking, so the operator can naturally look around and lean in for depth perception exactly as if their own eyes were physically at the robot's location, while their arm/hand motion is separately mirrored onto the robot's arms and hands.

**Method & training procedure.** The operator wears an Apple Vision Pro; a ZED Mini stereo camera sits on a 2–3-DOF actuated neck/gimbal mounted on the robot (tested on both a Unitree H1 and a Fourier GR-1 humanoid), whose motors are driven directly by the operator's tracked head pose, streaming stereo video back to the headset at 60 Hz. Wrist poses map to end-effector targets via closed-loop inverse kinematics; finger motions retarget to the robot's hand via an optimization-based retargeting library. Downstream policies used ACT with a pretrained DINOv2 vision-transformer backbone in place of the usual ResNet.

**Results.** Policy success rates of 87–100% across four long-horizon manipulation tasks (sorting, insertion, folding, unloading). Ablations make the paper's central claim directly testable: removing stereo vision (switching to a single monocular camera) dropped one task's pick success from 92% to 46% — roughly halving it. A **human** teleoperation user study (not just the trained policy) found 100% teleoperation success across all four tasks with stereo vision, versus dropping to 50–71% success on two tasks with monocular vision only — evidence that active stereo viewing helps the *human operator's* own performance, not only the downstream learned policy.

**How it connects.** This paper's core lesson generalizes past its specific hardware: what the demonstrator can *see* while teleoperating is itself part of the data-collection pipeline's design space, alongside how their motion is tracked (§6.4, §6.6) and what representation their motion is stored in (§6.2, §6.3).

### 6.6 Passive, cross-platform exoskeletons

A final teleoperation design point, sitting between the fully mechanical leader-follower rig (§6.2) and pure camera-based tracking (§6.4): a wearable mechanical linkage that is *not* motorized (it doesn't push back against the operator or hold their arm's weight) but is instrumented to measure the operator's own joint angles as they move naturally.

### Paper Breakdown: ACE (Cross-Platform Visual-Exoskeleton)

*Citation: Shiqi Yang, Minghuan Liu, Yuzhe Qin, Runyu Ding, Jialong Li, Xuxin Cheng, Ruihan Yang, Sha Yi, Xiaolong Wang, "ACE: A Cross-Platform and Visual-Exoskeletons System for Low-Cost Dexterous Teleoperation," CoRL 2024.*

**Problem/motivation.** Existing teleoperation interfaces generally have to trade off cost, ease of setup, and cross-robot reusability against each other — a system tuned to be cheap and easy to use is usually built or calibrated for one specific robot embodiment (§1) and needs hardware redesign to control a different arm, hand, or robot body plan.

**Key idea/innovation.** A single, cheap ($600), passive wearable mechanical arm — instrumented only with position-sensing servos (no motors pushing back) plus two ordinary wrist-mounted webcams for finger tracking — measures the operator's wrist pose via forward kinematics (§1) and finger pose via camera-based hand tracking, then maps that measured pose, through a general optimization-based retargeting layer, into the action space of essentially *any* downstream robot — the same physical rig reused across five very different robot configurations with no hardware changes, only a different software mapping per robot.

**Method & training procedure.** A 3D-printed, two-arm exoskeleton (7 links, 6 DOF per arm), each joint fitted with a Dynamixel servo used purely as a high-resolution angle *encoder* (not a motor) — the human's own arm moves the passive linkage, and the servos just report the resulting angles, from which wrist pose is computed via forward kinematics. Two low-cost webcams near the wrist, processed with the lightweight MediaPipe hand-tracking library, give 21 finger keypoints per hand for grip/finger control. The whole rig dons in under 30 seconds via magnetic connectors and adjusts to different arm lengths in under 2 minutes. Retargeting uses a linear scale-and-recenter formula for wrist position (rescaling the human's reachable workspace onto the robot's own, possibly differently-sized, workspace) and, for full dexterous hands, an optimization minimizing the gap between human and robot fingertip positions subject to the robot hand's joint limits. Validated across five robot platforms spanning a fixed arm with a simple gripper, two different humanoid-plus-dexterous-hand combinations, and a quadruped-mounted arm.

**Results.** Average forward-kinematics tracking error of about 1 mm, and 3 mm error in a controlled precision test. Head-to-head against GELLO (a prior low-cost, similarly passive rig): ACE was generally faster to reach targets and more successful (e.g. 97.1% vs. 47.6% success on one small-workspace condition), though not universally — GELLO edged out ACE on one medium-workspace condition, a genuine nuance rather than a clean sweep. Downstream imitation-learning tasks (3D Diffusion Policy or ACT, depending on platform) reached roughly 79–98% success across six tested tasks. Cost comparison the paper reports directly: ACE and GELLO both around $600, versus $20k for ALOHA (§6.2), $32k for Mobile ALOHA, and $4k for a comparable glove-based system (DexCap).

**How it connects.** ACE is the clearest illustration in this section of the "cost vs. cross-platform generality" trade-off named in its own motivation — it and Bunny-VisionPro (§6.4) both explicitly react against leader-follower teleoperation's robot-specificity, arriving at two different technical answers (passive mechanical sensing vs. markerless vision) to the same design question.

## 7. Beyond single-action regression: representing uncertainty and predicting ahead

### 7.1 Why an ordinary regression policy can fail even with perfect data

Go back to §2's plain BC formulation: a network `π_θ(o) → a` trained with an ordinary regression loss (mean-squared error) to predict the expert's action. This works fine when, for a given observation, there's essentially one reasonable action to take. It breaks down when there are **multiple, genuinely different valid actions** for the same observation — for instance, an expert who sometimes goes left around an obstacle and sometimes goes right, both equally fine, depending on nothing visible in the current observation (maybe it depended on which way the demonstrator happened to be leaning at the time).

A regression loss like MSE is minimized by the **conditional mean** of the target distribution. If the training data contains both "go left" and "go right" for visually identical observations, MSE training pushes the network toward the *average* of those two actions — which, for steering around an obstacle, is "go straight into it": worse than either individually-valid choice. This is called **mode collapse/averaging**, and it's a structural limitation of any policy architecture built as a single continuous regression function — no amount of extra training data fixes it, because the problem is the *shape* of function the architecture can represent, not a lack of data.

### 7.2 Mixture Density Networks: predicting a distribution instead of one action

§7.1's problem is that a regression head can only emit one action. The most direct architectural fix — predating both of this section's other two approaches — is to change what the output layer predicts: instead of an action vector, predict the parameters of a **[Mixture Density Network (MDN)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_model)**, a Gaussian mixture distribution *over* actions. This idea (Bishop, 1994, for regression problems generally) is used here as a drop-in replacement for the final layer of an otherwise-ordinary policy network.

**Structure.** Keep the same trunk (a CNN or MLP encoder of the observation) as any other policy in this section, but replace the final action-prediction layer with three small output heads that together parameterize a Gaussian mixture with K components: (1) **K mixture weights** `π_1, ..., π_K`, produced through a softmax so they sum to 1 and read as "how likely this component is the right mode"; (2) **K mean vectors** `μ_1, ..., μ_K`, one candidate action per mode; (3) **K (co)variances** `Σ_1, ..., Σ_K`, one spread per mode (commonly diagonal, parameterized through something like an exponential or softplus so the output stays positive).

**Input, output, label, and loss.** Input is the same observation `o` any policy in this section takes. Output is the full set of mixture parameters `{π_k, μ_k, Σ_k}` for k = 1..K — not an action. The training label is still just the single expert action `a_expert` actually demonstrated: there is no "which mode" label anywhere in the data, since which mode a given expert action belongs to is entirely latent, inferred implicitly as a side effect of training. The loss is the negative log-likelihood of the expert action under the predicted mixture density, not MSE:

```
loss = −log( Σ_k π_k · N(a_expert; μ_k, Σ_k) )
```

Minimizing this pushes probability mass onto wherever expert actions actually land — including, if the data genuinely contains two separated clusters of valid actions, two different mixture components — rather than collapsing them to their average the way an MSE regression loss does.

**Inference.** At test time, either sample an action from the predicted mixture, or take the mean of whichever component has the largest weight `π_k` — a quick way to read off "the most likely mode" without sampling noise.

**Why it's innovative.** An MDN lets a single feedforward network express a genuinely multimodal output distribution — several distinct, separately-plausible actions for the same observation — instead of committing to one point estimate. This directly attacks §7.1's failure mode architecturally: rather than changing the loss's target or searching at inference time, it simply gives the output layer enough structure to describe more than one peak.

**Why it wasn't enough in practice.** A small, fixed K turns out to be brittle, in a way distinct from §7.1's failure mode. §7.1's problem is architectural: a single continuous regression function *cannot* represent two separated valid actions, no matter how it's trained. An MDN's output layer *can* represent them — but gradient descent on the NLL loss above doesn't reliably learn to spread responsibility evenly across all K components. Early in training, whichever component happens to land closest to a cluster of expert actions dominates the sum inside the log (its exponential term is largest), so it keeps absorbing most of the gradient signal and improving further, while the other components — contributing almost nothing to that sum — receive vanishingly little gradient and drift toward small, uninformative weights. The net effect is one or two "winning" modes capture the dominant behavior while the rest collapse toward near-zero weight, rather than the mixture partitioning the action space among genuinely different, comparably-weighted modes. This is a *training-dynamics* flavor of mode collapse — components starving each other of gradient signal during optimization — distinct from §7.1's *architectural* flavor, where the function class itself cannot represent multiple modes regardless of how it's trained.

This is exactly the failure mode behind the "LSTM-GMM" baseline named only by score in §7.4's Results paragraph below — "GMM" there is precisely this Gaussian-mixture output head, wrapped around an LSTM trunk instead of a feedforward one, and its weak results on multimodal tasks are a direct symptom of the mode-starvation problem just described. It's exactly this brittleness — a small, fixed number of modes that collapses under ordinary gradient training — that motivated the two alternatives covered next: §7.3's energy-based policies, which sidestep committing to any fixed number of modes by scoring the entire action space with one function evaluated many times at inference, and §7.4's diffusion policies, which represent multimodality implicitly through an iterative denoising process rather than any explicit, mode-counted parameterization.

### 7.3 Energy-based policies, taught as one family

§7.2's Mixture Density Network fixes multimodality by committing, up front, to a fixed number `K` of Gaussian modes — and, as just shown, gradient descent doesn't reliably keep all `K` of them alive. The next family of methods, **energy-based policies**, sidesteps committing to any fixed number of modes at all, by changing what the network computes rather than how many outputs it has. This subsection builds the whole family from the ground up: first the network's structure (a genuinely different input/output shape from anything seen so far in this course), then the training signal that makes that structure learnable, and only then the two required-reading papers — Implicit Behavioral Cloning (IBC), which introduced the idea, and Ranking NCE, which found and fixed a hidden statistical bias in how IBC trains.

**Architecture Deep Dive: from an explicit regression network to an energy-based network.**

Every policy network seen so far in this section — plain BC in §2, the MDN in §7.2 — shares the same two-piece structure: an **encoder** (a CNN if the observation is an image, or the raw/lightly-processed state vector if it's already low-dimensional) turns the observation `o` into a feature vector, and an **MLP head** turns that feature vector into whatever the output is (an action, or a set of mixture parameters). Call this shape an *explicit* policy network: `o` goes in, and a description of the action comes directly out the other end.

An energy-based policy reuses exactly the same two pieces — encoder, then MLP — but rewires what goes in and what comes out:

> Picture a figure-skating judge. Rather than being asked to invent the best possible routine from scratch, the judge is handed one specific routine that was actually skated and asked only "how good was this one?" — a single score, low meaning good. Do this for many candidate routines, and picking out the best-scored one tells you which routine was best, without the judge ever having had to generate a routine itself. An energy-based network plays exactly this judge's role for actions.

Formally, an **[energy-based model (EBM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy-based_model)** used as a policy is a network `E_θ(o, a)` whose *input* is the encoded observation **concatenated with** a candidate action `a` — the candidate action becomes part of the input, not the output — and whose *output* is a single scalar number, the **energy**, where low energy means "this `(o, a)` pairing looks like something the expert would do." Side by side against the explicit network already familiar from §2:

| | Explicit BC network (§2) | Energy-based network (EBM) |
|---|---|---|
| Input | encoded `o` | encoded `o`, concatenated with a candidate `a` |
| Output | an action vector `â`, read directly off the output layer | one scalar, the energy `E_θ(o, a)` |
| How an action is obtained | forward pass produces it directly | search over candidate `a` for the one minimizing `E_θ(o, a)`: `â = argmin_a E_θ(o, a)` |

Only once this input/output swap is clear is it worth returning to *why* it helps. §7.1's regression head could only ever trace one continuous surface through action-space as a function of `o`, so it was structurally forced to interpolate — average — between two separated valid actions. An energy network faces no such constraint: because `argmin_a E_θ(o, a)` does not itself have to be a continuous function of `o`, the same encoder-plus-MLP machinery can instead carve the action space into two separate low-energy "valleys," one centered on each valid action, with a high-energy ridge in between where no expert ever acted. Picking the argmin means picking whichever valley is closest, so the policy can output either "go left" or "go right" cleanly — never their average — depending on which valley a given inference-time search happens to land in.

**Negative sampling: how do you train a network that only ever outputs a score?**

An energy network's output isn't an action, so it can't be trained with an ordinary regression loss (there's nothing to compute MSE against). Building the training signal from scratch, starting from something already familiar from ordinary supervised learning:

Recall ordinary **[softmax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Softmax_function)**/**[cross-entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy)** classification over a fixed set of classes: a network outputs one score per class, softmax turns the scores into a probability distribution over classes, and the loss pushes probability mass onto whichever class is correct. Now imagine a specific, artificial version of that setup: `K+1` "classes," where class 0 is "this is the actual action the expert took" and classes 1 through `K` are `K` randomly-sampled decoy actions that the expert did *not* take. Scoring each of the `K+1` candidates with the energy network and running ordinary softmax cross-entropy over that set — pushing the true action's score higher (lower energy) than every decoy's — is exactly IBC's training procedure. The decoys are called **negative samples** (the true action is the sole "positive"); training the network to tell positives from negatives this way is called a **contrastive loss**, and this specific instance of it is known as **[InfoNCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrastive_learning)**.

> **Worked example.** Suppose `K = 4`: the batch handed to the loss for one training pair is `{a_expert, a_neg1, a_neg2, a_neg3, a_neg4}`, five candidate actions total. The energy network scores all five, softmax turns the five (negated) energies into a 5-way probability distribution, and cross-entropy pushes probability mass onto slot 0 (`a_expert`) — precisely 5-way classification, just with 4 of the 5 "classes" freshly resampled at random on every training step instead of being fixed categories.

*Why decoys at all, rather than a direct probability?* A proper probability distribution over actions would need `p(a|o) = exp(−E(o,a)) / Z(o)`, where `Z(o) = ∫ exp(−E(o,a)) da` is a normalizing integral over the *entire, continuous, unbounded* action space — a quantity with no closed form and no tractable way to compute for an arbitrary neural network. Negative sampling sidesteps ever needing `Z(o)`: the network is never asked "what's the true probability of this action," only the far cheaper, comparative question "is this the real action, or one of these `K` sampled decoys?" That comparative question is all IBC's loss actually trains.

This is exactly IBC's loss, written out formally in its Paper Breakdown below — but naming it surfaces the one design choice glossed over above: *where do the `K` negative samples come from?* Sampling them **uniformly at random** from the action space is the simplest choice, and the one IBC uses. It also sets up the exact problem the second Paper Breakdown below (Ranking NCE) fixes: which distribution the negatives are drawn from silently determines what quantity the trained energy function actually converges to. In a high-dimensional action space, uniform sampling wastes almost every draw on decoys nowhere near anything plausible, which is practically unworkable — but switching to a smarter, non-uniform proposal distribution, without correcting the loss to account for it, turns out to make the learned energy function statistically **biased**: it stops converging to (a monotonic function of) the true `p(a|o)` at all.

### Paper Breakdown: Implicit Behavioral Cloning (IBC)

*Citation: Pete Florence, Corey Lynch, Andy Zeng, Oscar Ramirez, Ayzaan Wahid, Laura Downs, Adrian Wong, Johnny Lee, Igor Mordatch, Jonathan Tompson, "Implicit Behavioral Cloning," CoRL 2021.*

**Problem/motivation.** Exactly §7.1's mode-averaging failure: standard "explicit" policies, `â = F_θ(o)`, are continuous functions of the observation, and a continuous function *cannot* jump cleanly between two separated, both-valid actions or represent a genuine discontinuity — it necessarily interpolates through the invalid region in between.

**Key idea/innovation.** Train an energy-based network `E_θ(o, a)`, structured exactly as described in the Architecture Deep Dive above, and predict by minimizing: `â = argmin over a of E_θ(o, a)`. The paper proves this formally — any set-valued, discontinuous target mapping can be exactly represented as the argmin of some continuous scalar energy function, even though it cannot be represented by a single continuous direct-regression function — which is why implicit policies can represent exactly the discontinuous, multi-valued mappings explicit regression cannot.

**Method & training procedure.** IBC's training loss is precisely the uniform-negative-sampling InfoNCE loss derived above, with negatives drawn uniformly at random from the action space:

```
loss = −log[  exp(−E(o, a_expert))
             ─────────────────────────────────────────
             exp(−E(o, a_expert)) + Σ over negatives of exp(−E(o, a_negative))  ]
```

Since there's no closed-form way to directly compute `argmin_a E_θ(o,a)` for an arbitrary neural network, inference uses **[derivative-free optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative-free_optimization)**: start with many randomly-sampled candidate actions, repeatedly re-weight and re-sample them toward the lowest-energy candidates (shrinking the sampling noise each round, much like the **[cross-entropy method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-entropy_method)**), and return the best candidate after a few rounds — or, for higher-dimensional action spaces, a gradient-based **[Langevin-dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langevin_dynamics)** sampler that does use the energy function's gradient.

**Results.** On simulated block-pushing tasks, the implicit (energy-based) policy reached ~99–100% success across single-target, multi-target/multimodal, and pixel-observation variants, while an explicit MSE-trained policy dropped to 87–90% on the harder variants and a Gaussian-mixture baseline collapsed to 10% on pixel observations. On real-robot precision tasks the gap was much larger: on a tight 1mm-tolerance insertion task, implicit BC reached 83.3% success versus only 6.7% for explicit BC — roughly an order of magnitude better. A separate experiment varying action-space dimensionality with a fixed dataset size found implicit policies stayed at 95%+ success up to 16 action dimensions, versus only 8 for explicit policies — evidence implicit models also generalize better from limited data, not only handle multimodality.

**How it connects.** IBC's own training recipe turned out to have a subtle statistical flaw — exactly the uniform-vs-non-uniform negative-sampling issue flagged at the end of the Negative Sampling subsection above — fixed next by the Ranking-NCE paper. IBC's "predict via argmin of a learned function" framing is also a conceptual cousin of the score/energy-gradient view of diffusion sampling introduced in §7.4.

### Paper Breakdown: Revisiting Energy-Based Models as Policies (Ranking NCE / Interpolating EBMs)

*Citation: Sumeet Singh, Stephen Tu, Vikas Sindhwani, arXiv:2309.05803, 2023 (published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2024).*

**Problem/motivation.** Follow-up work using IBC repeatedly found its energy-based policies unstable and underperforming, without a clear mathematical explanation. This paper proves the reason is **not** merely "negative sampling is hard to tune" — it's that **IBC's training objective is statistically biased even with infinite data**, whenever the negative-action samples aren't drawn uniformly at random.

**Key idea/innovation.** IBC's contrastive loss is mathematically only unbiased for a *uniform* negative-sampling distribution — but as the Negative Sampling subsection above set up, uniform sampling is hopelessly inefficient in higher-dimensional action spaces (the vast majority of random samples are nowhere near anything useful), so practical implementations quietly need a smarter, non-uniform proposal distribution, which then makes the learned energy function converge to the wrong quantity: not the true action distribution `p(a|o)`, but the *ratio* `p(a|o) / (proposal distribution)`. The fix ("Ranking NCE," adapting an idea from Ma & Collins 2018): add an explicit correction term for the known density of the negative-sampling proposal directly into the same softmax-style loss, which restores an unbiased estimate for **any** proposal distribution, uniform or not — enabling a second, *learned* generative model to serve as an efficient proposal/negative-sampler, trained jointly but non-adversarially (via its own separate maximum-likelihood objective, not a GAN-style minimax game against the energy model).

**Method & training procedure.** The corrected loss (contrast this against IBC's loss above — the only difference is the `− log(proposal density)` correction term subtracted inside each exponential):

```
loss = −log[  exp(E(o,a_true) − log p_proposal(a_true|o))
             ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
             Σ over {a_true, negatives} of exp(E(o,a) − log p_proposal(a|o))  ]
```

The paper proves this is unbiased regardless of which proposal distribution is used (whereas plain IBC is only unbiased for a uniform one), and separately proves it approaches the statistical efficiency of full maximum-likelihood estimation as the number of negative samples grows. Training alternates: fit the proposal (a **[normalizing-flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow-based_generative_model)**-style generative model) via its own likelihood objective on the data, then fit the energy model via the corrected Ranking-NCE loss using fresh negatives drawn from that proposal — no adversarial min-max game, unlike some prior attempts to jointly train an EBM with a learned sampler. A second contribution, "**Interpolating Energy Models**," borrows the *multi-noise-scale* training idea that makes diffusion models (§7.4) work well: rather than one energy function, train a single energy function indexed by a continuous noise-scale variable (mirroring a diffusion process's interpolation between pure noise and the true action distribution), letting inference start cheaply from the easy, near-Gaussian noise-scale end and only invoke the more expensive learned energy landscape as it approaches the true, clean-data end.

**Results.** On synthetic 2D distributions, plain IBC's sample quality visibly lagged a normalizing flow, ordinary diffusion, and Ranking NCE (**[Bhattacharyya-coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhattacharyya_distance)** scores of 0.54–0.87 for IBC vs. 0.99+ for the others) — direct confirmation of the predicted bias. On a simulated obstacle-avoidance path-planning benchmark, Ranking NCE achieved both the lowest collision rate and lowest path cost of every method tested, including diffusion — notably, an ablation ranking multiple candidate action samples at inference time and keeping the best (something the energy/likelihood-based methods can do naturally, but a plain diffusion or CVAE policy structurally cannot do as reliably) showed sizeable gains, underscoring a genuine practical advantage of the energy/likelihood framing. On the same contact-rich Push-T manipulation benchmark used to evaluate Diffusion Policy (§7.4), the **Interpolating** variant of Ranking NCE outperformed plain (non-interpolating) Ranking NCE, ordinary diffusion, and a normalizing flow alike — the one task in the paper's results where the multi-noise-scale machinery was shown to be necessary, not just helpful.

**How it connects.** This paper closes the loop opened earlier in this section: it doesn't propose a new *kind* of policy so much as repair the training procedure originally proposed for implicit/energy-based policies, and shows that once repaired, energy-based methods are competitive with (and on some metrics, ahead of) the diffusion-based approach from §7.4 — while additionally being able to *rank* multiple candidate actions at inference time in a way diffusion and CVAE-based policies (§7.4) cannot as naturally.

### 7.4 Predicting further ahead: action-chunking architectures, taught as one family

**Shared framing: predicting a chunk of future actions, not one action.**

§6.1 introduced action chunking's basic idea — predict several future actions per forward pass instead of one — as a way to shorten the effective decision horizon and blunt §3.2's T²-type compounding error. Two of this week's readings, **ACT** (introduced alongside the ALOHA hardware in §6.2) and **Diffusion Policy**, are the actual architectures that implement this idea, and they're taught together here because they share an identical input/output contract and differ only in *how* they generate their output:

- **Input:** recent observations — camera images plus proprioception/joint state (§1) — covering a short window of the immediate past (ACT: the current timestep; Diffusion Policy: a short observation horizon, default the last 2 steps).
- **Output:** a **chunk** — an ordered sequence of several future actions (ACT: k = 100 actions; Diffusion Policy: a prediction horizon of 16 actions by default) — produced in a single forward pass, not one action at a time.
- **At execution time:** only the first several actions of the predicted chunk actually run on the robot before it re-observes and re-plans a fresh chunk — the **[receding-horizon control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_predictive_control)** pattern already named in §6.1.

**Why temporal ordering must be tracked explicitly.** A chunk is an ordered *sequence* of future timesteps, not an unordered bag of actions — the network has to know which output slot corresponds to "one step from now" versus "fifty steps from now," or the robot would have no way to tell which predicted action to run first. Both architectures below solve this with an explicit **positional encoding**: a signal fed alongside each output slot that marks its place in the sequence, so the same underlying network machinery (attention layers for ACT, a shared denoising network for Diffusion Policy) can still tell timestep 1 apart from timestep 50 even though it processes all slots with the same weights.

This ordering requirement resurfaces, more subtly, when two *overlapping* chunks — predicted at two different past moments — get combined into one continuous action stream (ACT's temporal ensembling; Diffusion Policy's replanning cadence). Suppose a chunk predicted 3 steps ago and a chunk predicted just now both contain a prediction for the *current* timestep — but that prediction sits at position 4 within the older chunk and position 1 within the newer one. Combining them correctly means aligning by each prediction's **absolute timestep** (which real moment in time it's a prediction *for*), never by its position within its own chunk — two predictions at the "same position" in two different chunks can refer to completely different real moments, and averaging by position instead of absolute time would silently blend predictions for unrelated timesteps together. ACT's temporal-ensembling weights (below) and Diffusion Policy's chunk-boundary handling both are built around this absolute-timestep alignment rule.

**From autoencoders to conditional variational autoencoders: the building block ACT's generative core needs.**

ACT's decoder is trained as part of a **conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE)** — three unfamiliar words stacked together, each explained here from scratch, building only on the encoder-MLP/decoder-MLP machinery already familiar from every network so far in this course.

*Autoencoder, plain first.* Imagine compressing a photo down to a short list of numbers — its "essence" — small enough that most of the photo's redundant detail is gone, then trying to rebuild the original photo from just that short list. If the rebuilt photo looks close to the original, the short list must have captured whatever actually mattered about the photo. An **[autoencoder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autoencoder)** is exactly this idea built as a neural network: an **encoder** (the same encoder-MLP/CNN machinery used throughout this course) compresses an input down to a small vector — the **bottleneck** — and a **decoder** (another MLP, running the shape in reverse) tries to reconstruct the original input from just that bottleneck vector. The loss is simply reconstruction error (e.g. MSE between input and reconstruction). *Why this teaches the network anything:* the bottleneck is deliberately too small to just copy the input through unchanged, so the only way to get low reconstruction error is for the encoder to learn to keep whichever features of the input are actually needed to rebuild it, and discard the rest — exactly the behavior wanted from a compressor.

*Variational, as a second, separate step.* A plain autoencoder's bottleneck is one fixed vector per input — great for reconstructing inputs it has seen, but there's no principled way to pick a *new* bottleneck vector and get a valid, new output back out (nothing guarantees the space between two known bottleneck vectors decodes to anything sensible). A **[variational autoencoder (VAE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_autoencoder)** fixes this by making the encoder output the *parameters of a probability distribution* (a mean and variance) rather than one fixed vector, sampling the actual bottleneck vector from that distribution at training time, and adding a loss term that pulls every input's distribution toward a shared, simple reference distribution (a standard Gaussian). The effect: nearby points in the bottleneck space are forced to decode to similarly plausible outputs, because training has explicitly smoothed and packed the whole space together rather than leaving isolated islands per training example — which is exactly what makes it safe to sample a *new* bottleneck vector at test time and still get a valid, new decoded output, not just reconstruct inputs already seen.

*Conditional, as the last step.* A plain VAE decodes from the bottleneck alone, with no way to say "reconstruct the right output *for this particular situation*." A **conditional VAE (CVAE)** simply also feeds the decoder some side information — here, the current observation — so the decoder learns to produce "the right action chunk for this state," not just any plausible chunk in the abstract. Put together: encoder compresses (true future chunk + state) into a sampled latent `z` at training time; decoder, conditioned on both `z` and the observation, reconstructs the chunk; at test time, with no true future chunk available to compress, `z` is simply fixed to its reference value (zero) and the decoder runs directly off the observation alone.

**A primer on self-attention and transformers, since ACT's decoder is one.**

Every network architecture used so far in this course — a CNN encoder, an MLP head, even the MDN's mixture heads — processes each input the same fixed way regardless of what else is present in the input; a CNN's convolution filter, for instance, is applied identically no matter what's elsewhere in the image. That's fine when the input is a single, self-contained thing (one image, one state vector) but poorly suited to a *sequence* of related elements — such as a chunk's output slots, or a set of camera-image patches, or a joint-state reading — where what each element should do genuinely depends on the *other* elements present, not just on itself.

**[Self-attention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_(machine_learning))** solves exactly this: rather than transforming each sequence element in isolation through fixed weights, it lets every element look at, compare itself against, and weight its contribution from *every other element* in the sequence — the weighting itself is computed from the sequence's own content, so which elements matter most to which others changes example by example, rather than being fixed at training time. A **[transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_(deep_learning_architecture))** is a network built by stacking layers of self-attention (plus ordinary per-element MLPs) instead of convolution or fixed-weight layers.

In an ordinary text transformer, the sequence elements ("tokens") are words or word-fragments. In ACT, the role a word plays is instead filled by one timestep's worth of information: a camera-image patch embedding, the joint-state reading, the latent `z`, or one output chunk-slot — each is a "token" the transformer's self-attention lets look at every other token (e.g. letting the prediction for timestep 50 attend directly to the current camera image and to the prediction for timestep 49) before producing its own output.

**A primer on denoising diffusion, since Diffusion Policy is one.**

*What does it even mean to train a network to remove noise?* Imagine taking a clean photo and, over many steps, adding a little random static (noise) at each step, until after enough steps the "photo" is indistinguishable from pure random static — no trace of the original left. Now train a network to do one step of this process *in reverse*: shown a somewhat-noisy image and told which step of the noising process it's at, predict what noise was just added, so subtracting the network's guess partially cleans the image up. This is **[denoising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model)**: a single, ordinary regression network (predict a noise vector, trained with MSE against the actual noise that was added — no exotic loss required) applied to a mildly harder-than-usual prediction problem.

*Why running this many times, starting from pure noise, produces something new.* One denoising step only removes a little noise, and (crucially) the network was only ever trained on how to partially clean up images that started as *real* photos and were noised a controlled amount — so a single step from pure random static wouldn't work well. But running the trained denoising step **repeatedly**, starting from pure noise and gradually working backward through the same noise levels used in training, lets each step nudge the current (very noisy, then progressively less noisy) sample back toward *something* the network recognizes as plausible — and because the starting point was random noise, not any one specific real photo, the specific plausible image the process settles into is genuinely new each run, not a memorized reconstruction. This iterative, many-step reverse process — training in the noise-adding direction, generating in the noise-removing direction — is what's named **[DDPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model)** (denoising diffusion probabilistic models); **DDIM**, used at inference time below, is a faster variant that skips some of the reverse steps without retraining.

Bridged into this domain: what's being noised and denoised here is not an image's pixels but a **chunk of future actions** — the "photo" is a sequence of action vectors, noise is added to the whole chunk, and the trained network predicts what noise was added, conditioned on the current observation (which is never itself noised).

### Paper Breakdown: ACT (Action Chunking Transformer)

*Citation: Tony Z. Zhao, Vikash Kumar, Sergey Levine, Chelsea Finn, "Learning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware," RSS 2023 — the same paper that introduces the ALOHA hardware covered in §6.2.*

**Problem/motivation.** Beyond ALOHA's hardware-cost problem (§6.2), fine-grained bimanual manipulation policies trained via ordinary single-action-at-a-time BC suffer two compounding issues: §3.2's compounding error over long horizons, and §6.1's non-stationarity problem — real human teleoperation demonstrations contain pauses and hesitations that confuse a policy predicting only the very next action about whether to pause or continue.

**Key idea/innovation.** Combine action chunking (predict `k = 100` future actions per forward pass, shrinking the effective decision horizon per §6.1) with a **conditional-generative decoder** — a CVAE, per the primer above — so the network can represent the genuine variability across different demonstrated chunks (different hesitation patterns, different pacing) rather than averaging them into one blurry, invalid chunk the way an ordinary regression decoder would (§7.1's mode-averaging argument, now applied to whole action sequences rather than single actions).

**Method & training procedure.** ACT is trained as the decoder half of a CVAE (see the autoencoder → CVAE primer above for what each of "autoencoder," "variational," and "conditional" is doing here). During training, a transformer encoder (see the self-attention primer above) compresses the true future action chunk plus the current joint state into a latent **style variable `z`**, capturing exactly the kind of demonstration variability the primer's smoothing argument lets a VAE's latent absorb. A transformer decoder, conditioned on `z`, the four camera images, and the current joint state, then outputs a chunk of the next **k = 100** actions in a single forward pass: self-attention lets each predicted timestep's output slot attend to every input token (images, joint state, `z`) and to the other output slots, with a positional encoding (per the Shared Framing subsection above) marking each slot's place in the sequence. At test time the encoder is discarded and `z` is fixed to zero (there is no true future chunk to compress), and the decoder runs open-loop over its predicted chunk, with **temporal ensembling**: overlapping chunks predicted at different past timesteps are combined via an exponentially-weighted average (`weight ∝ exp(−m · how_far_in_the_past)`, so more recent predictions dominate while jitter/noise gets smoothed out) — each contributing prediction aligned by its absolute timestep, exactly the alignment rule the Shared Framing subsection flags as necessary.

**Results.** Six real tasks, 50 demonstrations each (100 for the hardest, "Thread Velcro"): success rates from 20% (Thread Velcro) up to 96% (Slot Battery), with the two showcase tasks (opening a translucent cup, slotting a battery) headlined in the abstract at "80–90%" (the paper's own detailed per-task table reports 84% for the cup and 96% for the battery — the abstract's range is an approximate summary, not each task's literal figure). Ablations isolate the two design choices cleanly: sweeping the chunk size from k=1 to k=100 raised success from 1% to 44% on one task in isolation, and removing the CVAE/generative component specifically hurt performance on *noisy, human-collected* data (35.3% → 2%) while making almost no difference on noise-free scripted data — direct evidence the CVAE is earning its keep on real human demonstration noise specifically, not just generically helpful.

**How it connects.** ACT pairs with the ALOHA hardware described in §6.2 to reach the success rates just listed. Contrasted immediately below against Diffusion Policy, which solves the identical "predict a chunk of future actions" problem via iterative denoising rather than ACT's single-shot conditional decode — two different generative mechanisms aimed at the same target.

### Paper Breakdown: Diffusion Policy

*Citation: Cheng Chi, Zhenjia Xu, Siyuan Feng, Eric Cousineau, Yilun Du, Benjamin Burchfiel, Russ Tedrake, Shuran Song, "Diffusion Policy: Visuomotor Policy Learning via Action Diffusion," RSS 2023.*

**Problem/motivation.** IBC (§7.3) fixes mode-averaging but is finicky to train — its contrastive loss needs a working negative-sampling scheme to approximate an otherwise-intractable normalization term, and this negative sampling is a known source of instability, especially as action dimensionality grows. Simpler multimodal alternatives (Gaussian-mixture output heads) tend to collapse toward a single mode in practice rather than faithfully representing genuine multimodality.

**Key idea/innovation.** Formulate action prediction as a **[diffusion model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model)** (see the denoising-diffusion primer above): rather than an energy function evaluated once, train a network to iteratively **denoise** a sequence of future actions, starting from pure random noise, conditioned on recent observations — sidestepping IBC's negative-sampling instability (denoising is trained with an ordinary, stable regression loss) while still representing arbitrarily multimodal action distributions, because the iterative noisy refinement process can settle into different valid modes on different runs, the same way image-generating diffusion models produce varied outputs from the same text prompt. This is a structurally different generative mechanism from ACT's CVAE (above): ACT reaches its output through one **single-shot conditional decode** (encode variability into `z`, decode once), while Diffusion Policy reaches the same kind of output — a chunk of future actions — through **iterative denoising**, many small refinement passes rather than one decode. Both solve the identical "predict a chunk" problem; the two Paper Breakdowns above and below are worth reading back to back specifically to see how differently they get there.

**Method & training procedure.** Borrowing standard denoising-diffusion machinery: starting from Gaussian noise, a trained network `ε_θ` is applied repeatedly to progressively remove noise, converging on a clean sample. Here, "the sample" is a whole **chunk of future actions** (§6.1) rather than a single action, and the network is conditioned on a short history of recent observations (default: the last 2 steps) — critically, the observation conditioning is never itself noised; only the action chunk is. The chunking scheme uses three horizon parameters: an **observation horizon** (how much recent context the network sees, default 2 steps), a **prediction horizon** (how many future actions it predicts at once, default 16), and a shorter **execution horizon** (how many of those predicted actions actually get run before replanning, default 8) — this receding-horizon setup (§6.1) balances temporal smoothness against staying responsive to new observations; predicting too few actions ahead loses the smoothness benefit, predicting/committing too many makes the robot slow to react to surprises. The training loss is the standard diffusion objective, adapted to condition on observations:

```
loss = MSE( true_noise,  ε_θ(observation_history, noisy_action_chunk, diffusion_step) )
```

Two network variants were tested: a convolutional **[U-Net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U-Net)** (robust, works well "out of the box," but biased toward smooth/slow-changing action sequences) and a transformer (conditioned via cross-attention over the observation embeddings, better on fast-changing/complex tasks but more sensitive to tuning). At inference, **[DDIM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model#Denoising_Diffusion_Implicit_Model_%28DDIM%29)** sampling lets training use many denoising steps (100) while inference uses far fewer (as few as 10), keeping real-time latency low (~0.1s on a consumer GPU in their real-robot tests).

**Results.** Across 15 tasks spanning 4 benchmark suites (Robomimic, a 2D pushing task, a multimodal block-pushing task, and a long-horizon kitchen task), the paper reports an average 46.9% improvement over prior state-of-the-art baselines (an LSTM-GMM policy, IBC, and a Behavior Transformer). On the multimodal block-pushing stress test specifically, the transformer variant of Diffusion Policy reached 0.99/0.94 success on reaching the first/second target, dramatically ahead of LSTM-GMM (0.03/0.01) and IBC (0.01/0.00) — direct evidence the multimodality fix is doing real work, not just improving average performance. On a real-world pushing task, Diffusion Policy reached 95% success versus 20% for IBC and 0% for LSTM-GMM in the same setup. Ablations confirmed the action-horizon trade-off described above (too-short or too-long execution horizons both hurt) and found **position control** (predicting target end-effector positions) tolerates replanning delay much better than **velocity control**, tying back to §3.2's compounding-error framing.

**How it connects.** Diffusion Policy is the downstream policy architecture UMI (§6.3) actually trains on top of its embodiment-agnostic data. Together with ACT (above), it illustrates a recurring theme of this whole lecture: "predict many steps ahead" has more than one defensible generative-modeling answer, echoing the same design-space plurality §6 showed for teleoperation hardware itself.

## 8. Deciding when to query the expert: uncertainty-driven interactive imitation learning

**Two kinds of uncertainty.** When a learned policy is unsure what to do, it's worth distinguishing *why*:
- **[Epistemic uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_quantification)** ("model uncertainty") comes from not having seen enough data — e.g. flipping an unfamiliar biased coin a few times and being unsure of its true bias. This kind of uncertainty **shrinks toward zero as more data arrives**, in principle vanishing entirely with infinite data.
- **[Aleatoric uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_quantification)** ("observation noise") is irreducible: even knowing a coin's *exact* bias precisely, you still can't predict any single flip's outcome. No amount of additional data removes this kind of uncertainty — it's intrinsic to the process being modeled, not a gap in what's been learned.

Practically, estimating a neural network's epistemic uncertainty usually means approximating a distribution over the network's *weights* (rather than a single fixed weight setting) — via an ensemble of independently-trained networks, an approximate Bayesian technique like Monte Carlo dropout, or full Markov-chain sampling over weights — and asking how much the network's predictions vary across that distribution.

**Querying an expert only when necessary — five different answers.** DAgger (§4) assumes the expert is always available to label *every* visited state, every iteration — expensive in practice, and where MMD-IL (§5) already tried one distributional-distance heuristic for when to query, the five papers below tackle the identical underlying question — "given a rollout, which states are actually worth an expert query?" — with genuinely different notions of "worth it," ranging from a hand-tuned geometric heuristic to a fully theoretical, regret-bounded answer for a *noisy* expert. Read in the order given: SHIV and DropoutDAgger use two different cheap proxies for risk — one a geometric novelty/support measure, the other the epistemic-uncertainty estimate introduced just above — to decide when to query; SafeDAgger instead trains a wholly separate classifier whose only job is to predict when the primary policy is about to disagree with the expert; the Learning-to-Defer paper generalizes the same question beyond robotics entirely, to any human-AI collaboration setting, with a proof that its training loss is *consistent* (optimizing it is provably equivalent, in the limit, to optimizing the actual defer-or-not decision wanted); and the Selective Sampling paper drops this section's running assumption of a perfect expert altogether, handling deliberately *noisy* guidance and proving formal query-complexity bounds via a new complexity measure, the eluder dimension, built from scratch below.

### Paper Breakdown: SHIV (Support Vectors for Reducing Supervisor Burden)

*Citation: Michael Laskey, Sam Staszak, Wesley Yu-Shu Hsieh, Jeffrey Mahler, Florian T. Pokorny, Anca D. Dragan, Ken Goldberg, "SHIV: Reducing Supervisor Burden in DAgger Using Support Vectors for Efficient Learning from Demonstrations in High Dimensional State Spaces," ICRA 2016.*

**Problem/motivation.** Plain DAgger (§4) queries the expert for a label at *every* state a rollout visits — the paper's stated goal is to "reduce supervisor burden without degrading performance." Their diagnosis of what actually makes a state worth querying: "the robot only needs to request demonstrations for risky states. Risk arises because: (1) states are sufficiently different than the states previously encountered... or (2) states are in a region that has been misclassified in previous training data."

**Key idea/innovation.** Train a **[one-class support vector machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_vector_machine)** — not an ordinary two-class classifier, and not a distance-to-decision-boundary heuristic — to detect, geometrically, whether the current state looks like anything already reliably labeled, and query the expert only on states this detector flags as risky.

**Building "one-class SVM" from scratch.** An ordinary (two-class) **[support vector machine (SVM)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Support_vector_machine)** separates two labeled groups of points with the widest possible "street" (margin) between them, and classifies a new point by which side of that street it falls on — working directly with a handful of "support" training points and a fixed similarity function (a **kernel**) between any two points, rather than many layers of learned features the way a neural network does. A **one-class** SVM adapts this to a setting with no negative examples at all: given only a pile of "normal" points (here, states already reliably labeled), it draws the tightest boundary around them, treating anything falling well outside it as an outlier — without ever needing to explicitly estimate a full probability density over the state space (which, the paper notes, "scales exponentially in the dimension of the state space"). Picture a bouncer who's only ever seen the regular guests at a party: rather than checking every new arrival's ID against a fixed list, the bouncer has learned roughly what "typical regular guest" looks like, and flags anyone who looks noticeably different for a closer look — the one-class SVM plays exactly this "does this look like what I've already reliably seen?" role over robot states.

**Method & training procedure.** SHIV modifies DAgger's own aggregation step (§4.1): rather than labeling every visited state, it splits the states already labeled so far into two sets by whether the current policy's own prediction error on that state exceeds a threshold — a "correctly-handled" set and a "misclassified" set — and trains a *separate* one-class SVM boundary on each. A newly-visited state is deemed **risky**, and only then sent to the expert for a query, if it falls *outside* the correctly-handled set's boundary, *or* falls *inside* the misclassified set's boundary — i.e., "safe" means "looks like something already reliably handled, and doesn't look like a region the policy has historically gotten wrong." This "carving out holes" around known trouble spots, layered on top of plain novelty detection, is what distinguishes SHIV's specific risk measure. A single kernel-bandwidth parameter directly trades off query frequency against reliability: a small bandwidth flags almost everything as risky (many queries, very conservative), a large one flags almost nothing (few queries, more risk of missing a genuinely risky state) — the paper reports performance stays "similarly good" across a sizeable middle range of this parameter, calling the method "robust to the choice of a particular σ."

**Results.** Tested on three domains: a driving simulator (3 discrete steering actions, supervised by a search-based planner that itself crashes an average of 5.9 times per lap), a simulated robot arm pushing objects through clutter to grasp them, and real surgeon-demonstrated needle insertion on a da Vinci surgical robot. Averaged over many DAgger iterations and randomized trials, SHIV reached equivalent task performance to plain DAgger while needing a "71% reduction in the number of queries" (driving), "64% reduction" (grasping in clutter), and "67% reduction" (needle insertion) — headlined in the abstract as "up to 70% fewer queries" for comparable performance. In a direct comparison of risk-detection methods, SHIV's combined (novelty-plus-misclassification) measure mis-flagged genuinely risky states as safe at roughly half the error rate of a Maximum-Mean-Discrepancy-based detector — the same MMD tool from §5's MMD-IL paper, here used purely as a risk-detection baseline rather than for policy selection.

**How it connects.** SHIV's notion of "uncertainty" is a genuinely different flavor from the neural-network-based kind covered next: it never looks at a predictive *distribution* over actions at all, only at where a state sits, geometrically, relative to a kernel-based support boundary over previously-seen states — a "does this look familiar" question rather than a "how confident is my network" question. The paper builds explicitly on DAgger's own reduction (§4) and cites Ross & Bagnell's quadratic-error argument (§3.2) as the original motivation for querying interactively at all; DropoutDAgger, immediately below, tackles the identical "which states are risky" question with the network-uncertainty-based approach SHIV's authors deliberately avoid.

### Paper Breakdown: DropoutDAgger

*Citation: Kunal Menda, Katherine Driggs-Campbell, Mykel J. Kochenderfer, "DropoutDAgger: A Bayesian Approach to Safe Imitation Learning," arXiv:1709.06166, 2017.*

**Problem/motivation.** Plain DAgger's own decision rule for who's in control during a rollout is a coin flip with a fixed, decaying probability of using the expert — it has, in the paper's words, "no regard to any similarity measure between the novice and the expert choice of action," so it can hand control to the learner even when the learner's suggested action is dangerously different from the expert's. A prior fix (SafeDAgger, below) compares the novice's action to the expert's against one fixed distance threshold — but a *single* fixed threshold, the authors argue, is "either too conservative when the system is far from unsafe states or too relaxed when near them," because it never accounts for how *confident* the novice's own prediction actually is.

**Key idea/innovation.** Use **[Monte Carlo dropout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dropout_(neural_networks))** — running the same trained network many times on the same input, each time with a different randomly-sampled dropout mask switching off a different subset of units — as a cheap way to sample from an approximate Bayesian posterior over the network's own predictions, and let the novice act only when enough of these samples agree closely with what the expert would have done.

**Connecting back to "Two kinds of uncertainty," above.** This is a direct, concrete instance of the epistemic-uncertainty estimation technique already introduced at the top of this section: rather than trusting a single forward pass, repeatedly randomizing which units are active approximates asking "how would a range of slightly-different, equally-plausible versions of this trained network answer?" — if all the dropout-perturbed versions agree, that's read as low epistemic uncertainty (the network has genuinely learned a confident answer here); if they scatter widely, that's read as high epistemic uncertainty (not enough training data has pinned down what to do here). The paper's own guidance names both kinds of uncertainty from this section's opening explicitly, together: "the dropout probability *d* should be chosen to reflect the epistemic uncertainty, arising from finite demonstration data, and aleatoric uncertainty, arising from the stochastic environment."

**Method & training procedure.** The novice network is trained with ordinary dropout at every layer, on the same growing, DAgger-aggregated dataset as usual (§4.1). At each control step, the decision rule draws `N` independent forward passes of the novice network (each with an independently-sampled dropout mask) to get `N` candidate actions, and compares each to the expert's action at that state: if the fraction of the `N` candidates landing within a fixed distance `τ` of the expert's action is at least a threshold `p`, the novice acts (using the *average* of its `N` sampled actions); otherwise, control reverts to the expert. Three degenerate special cases make the design's logic clear: setting the dropout rate to 0 collapses this exactly to SafeDAgger's fixed-threshold rule (below); setting `τ` to 0 collapses it to plain behavioral cloning (§2.2) (never trusting the novice unless it matches the expert exactly); and setting `p` to 0 collapses it to always trusting the expert (pure passive labeling, no novice control at all).

**Results.** Tested on a simulated HalfCheetah continuous-control task (with a clean and a sensor-noise-corrupted observation variant) and a lidar-guided Dubins-car navigation task, comparing against plain behavioral cloning, an "always let the novice act" baseline, vanilla DAgger, and SafeDAgger. Unlike SHIV, the paper reports its results only as learning curves across DAgger training epochs rather than a single percentage-reduction number; its consistent qualitative finding is that DropoutDAgger "makes no compromise to the safety of the combined system" while the novice still "learns a well-performing policy as quickly as other algorithms" — and specifically under added observation noise (deliberately raising aleatoric uncertainty), DropoutDAgger was the only method among those tested whose safety performance wasn't degraded by the added noise.

**How it connects.** DropoutDAgger answers the same "when to query" question as SHIV above, using the exact kind of predictive-distribution uncertainty SHIV's authors set out to avoid — a useful, direct contrast in how differently "the learner is unsure" can be operationalized. It also generalizes SafeDAgger's fixed-threshold idea, immediately below, by additionally accounting for the novice's own confidence, not only its raw disagreement with the expert.

### Paper Breakdown: SafeDAgger

*Citation: Jiakai Zhang, Kyunghyun Cho, "Query-Efficient Imitation Learning for End-to-End Autonomous Driving," arXiv:1605.06450, 2016.* (This is the method the earlier draft of these notes named "SafeDAgger" without a citation — it gets its full citation and breakdown here.)

**Problem/motivation.** Plain DAgger fixes covariate shift by querying a reference (expert) policy at every visited state — but the reference policy is often itself expensive to run (a large planner, or a human), so querying it constantly is wasteful whenever the learner's current policy is already doing fine.

**Key idea/innovation.** Train a second, separate binary classifier — a "safety policy" — whose only job is to predict whether the primary policy's action would deviate too far from the reference policy's action at the current state, and query the reference policy (to collect a training label) only on states the safety policy flags as likely to deviate.

**Method & training procedure.** Deviation at a state is measured as squared distance between the primary policy's action and the reference policy's action, `ε(π, π*, s) = ||π(s) − π*(s)||²`; a state is labeled "unsafe" whenever this exceeds a fixed threshold τ. The safety policy is trained with ordinary binary cross-entropy against these unsafe/safe labels; the primary policy is trained with ordinary supervised regression against the reference policy's actions — both perfectly standard supervised losses, with the innovation being *which states* feed the primary policy's training set. The full loop: collect an initial dataset by running the reference policy, and train both an initial primary and an initial safety policy; then, for several iterations, drive using a mixture of the primary and reference policies (handing control to the reference policy whenever the safety policy flags "unsafe"), keep only the *flagged-unsafe* states from that rollout, add reference-policy labels for those states to the growing training set, and retrain both policies. In their driving-simulator experiments, the unsafe threshold τ was set so that "approximately 20% of initial training examples are considered unsafe."

**Results.** In the TORCS racing simulator, across seven training tracks and three held-out test tracks (with 40 traffic cars added per training track), SafeDAgger retained only "30k, 30k, and 10k" training examples per iteration after subset-selection, versus plain DAgger needing "up to 390k data each iteration" before downsampling to match — a large reduction in how much data actually needs labeling. The paper reports that "after the third iteration, the primary policy trained with the SafeDAgger is perfect," and that at test time the reference policy needed to step in only "7.11% and 10.81% of time without and with traffic," respectively.

**How it connects.** SafeDAgger answers the same "when to query" question as SHIV and DropoutDAgger above, but with yet another notion of "risky state" — not a distance-from-training-data heuristic, and not the learner's own predictive uncertainty, but a directly-trained, learned predictor of disagreement-with-the-expert. The paper notes explicitly that if the safety threshold τ is set high enough to call every state "safe," "the SafeDAgger reduces to the original DAgger" — making clear that SafeDAgger is best understood as a strict generalization of plain DAgger, not a separate algorithm.

### Paper Breakdown: Consistent Estimators for Learning to Defer to an Expert

*Citation: Hussein Mozannar, David Sontag, "Consistent Estimators for Learning to Defer to an Expert," ICML 2020.*

**Problem/motivation.** The three papers above all stay inside robotics/control, deciding when a *policy* should hand a *state* to a human expert. This paper asks the same underlying question in a completely different, much more general setting: ordinary supervised classification, where a machine-learning system is deployed alongside a human expert (e.g. flagging content for moderation, or triaging medical images) and must decide, per example, whether to predict itself or defer to the human — with no access to whatever private information the human expert's own judgment draws on.

**Key idea/innovation.** Jointly learn two things at once — an ordinary classifier `h` and a "rejector" (deferral) function `r` deciding whether to defer — by minimizing a single, carefully-designed surrogate loss that is *consistent*: provably, minimizing this surrogate recovers the actual best possible classifier-and-deferral pair (the pair that would be optimal if the real, non-smooth defer-or-not decision could be optimized directly), rather than merely something loosely related to it.

**Building "consistent surrogate loss" from scratch.** A **surrogate loss** is a smoother, easier-to-optimize stand-in for a loss actually cared about but hard to optimize directly — the same reason cross-entropy is used as a smooth stand-in for raw classification accuracy in ordinary supervised learning. A surrogate is called **consistent** for a given true objective if optimizing the surrogate, given enough data and a rich enough model, provably converges to the same solution optimizing the true objective would have found — i.e., the substitution is a free lunch, not a compromise. Here, the true objective wanted is a joint classify-or-defer decision rule minimizing total expected cost (a wrong self-prediction costs one thing, deferring to a possibly-wrong expert costs another); the paper's loss `L_CE` is proven, in their Theorem 1, to be exactly this: convex (so tractable to optimize), an upper bound on the true error, and consistent — its minimizer provably equals the mathematically-optimal classify-or-defer rule, which (their Proposition 2) works out to a clean, checkable form: predict the most probable class, unless the probability that the expert's own guess would be correct exceeds that — in which case, defer.

**Method & training procedure.** Concretely, `L_CE` reduces the joint problem to an ordinary, familiar (K+1)-class softmax cross-entropy: alongside the K real classes, add one extra "defer" class `⊥`. One term of the loss is the usual cross-entropy pushing probability toward the true label `y`; a second term rewards assigning probability to the "defer" class specifically when the human expert's guess `m` happens to equal the true label `y` — training the model to prefer deferring exactly on the kinds of examples the expert tends to get right. At test time, the classifier's prediction is whichever of the K real classes scores highest; the system defers whenever the "defer" class outscores every real class.

**Results.** Tested outside robotics entirely: on CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100 image classification with a synthetic expert (perfect on a subset of classes, random elsewhere), `L_CE` beat a confidence-based deferral baseline by "+1.01% system accuracy" on average on CIFAR-10 (e.g. 70.12% vs. 62.33% in a low-data setting) and "1.60% on average" on CIFAR-100; on CIFAR-10H (using real human-labeled confidence data as the expert) and a hate-speech-detection dataset, `L_CE`-based methods again outperformed the confidence baseline (e.g. 92.91% vs. 92.42% system accuracy on hate speech); and on CheXpert chest X-ray triage, `L_CE` methods dominated the confidence baseline across most operating points.

**How it connects.** This paper generalizes, well beyond robotics, the exact question §4–§8 have been asking about physical control policies — when should an automated decision-maker hand control to a human — into a fully general, theoretically-grounded classification setting; its "provably consistent surrogate loss" standard is a stricter theoretical bar than any of the three more heuristic/applied papers above meet, foreshadowing the fully theoretical treatment of noisy-expert querying in the Selective Sampling paper immediately below.

### Paper Breakdown: Selective Sampling and Imitation Learning via Online Regression

*Citation: Ayush Sekhari, Karthik Sridharan, Wen Sun, Runzhe Wu, "Selective Sampling and Imitation Learning via Online Regression," arXiv:2307.04998, 2023.*

**Problem/motivation.** Every method above — DAgger, MMD-IL, SHIV, DropoutDAgger, SafeDAgger — assumes the expert being queried is *correct*: whatever it says, that's the exact action to imitate. Real experts (human demonstrators especially) are noisy: the same expert, asked twice about the same state, might not give exactly the same answer. This paper asks: with a genuinely noisy expert, how few queries does an interactive imitation-learning algorithm need, and what theoretical guarantee can it still offer?

**Key idea/innovation.** Reduce "decide when to query a noisy expert" to a well-studied theoretical tool, **[online regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Online_machine_learning)** (repeatedly predicting a value, getting told the true value, and updating — the same online-learning family DAgger itself reduces to in §4.2, just for regression instead of classification/control), and query the expert only when the online regressor's own predicted margin (how confidently it favors one action over the runner-up) isn't comfortably larger than its own current uncertainty about that prediction.

**Building the eluder dimension from scratch.** The paper's bounds depend on a complexity measure for the space of possible models, the **eluder dimension** (no dedicated reference page exists for this term; it's a relatively specialized RL/bandit-theory concept), that doesn't appear elsewhere in these notes. Plain-language picture first: imagine a sequence of new questions, each one designed so that some function in a model class looks *very wrong* on that new question, yet every one of those same functions looked *nearly perfect* on every question asked so far. The eluder dimension is the longest such sequence of "gotcha" questions a model class can be forced through before it runs out of room to keep surprising you — formally, the length of the longest sequence of inputs `x_1, ..., x_m` such that at each step `i` there's some candidate function that matches the true function closely (within a fixed tolerance) on every earlier input `x_1, ..., x_{i-1}`, yet disagrees with it by more than that tolerance at `x_i`. A small eluder dimension means: once a model class has been consistent with the truth on a modest number of points, it can't keep being surprised — its remaining uncertainty is genuinely running out. (Finite model classes with `n` candidate functions have eluder dimension at most `n − 1`; simple linear-style model classes in `d` dimensions have eluder dimension `O(d)`.)

**Method & training procedure.** The algorithm maintains an online-regression predictor `f_t` of the (unknown) function generating the expert's noisy responses, together with a shrinking confidence radius `Δ_t` around that prediction (computed by a constrained-optimization calculation over which functions in the model class remain plausible given data seen so far). At each new state, it queries the expert only if the model's own predicted margin between its top choice and the runner-up is not clearly larger than twice that confidence radius — i.e., query only when the model genuinely isn't sure yet, not merely whenever the state looks unfamiliar the way SHIV/MMD-IL check. The paper proves two bounds: a regret bound (how much worse the learned policy performs than an idealized one) and a query-complexity bound (how many expert queries are needed in total) — with the eluder dimension entering specifically the *query-complexity* bound, not the regret bound, and a separate quantity capturing how often the expert *itself* is genuinely ambiguous (rather than the learner being unsure) also appearing in both.

**Results.** This is the most theoretical paper in this group, and its own authors do not report benchmark-style success-rate numbers the way, say, DAgger's or SafeDAgger's papers do. It separately proves that any *non-interactive* algorithm facing a noisy expert needs an exponential (in horizon) number of demonstrations to learn a good policy at all — a formal argument for why some interactive querying is unavoidable once the expert is noisy, not just a nice-to-have. Its one empirical section tests on a Cartpole simulation with single and multiple simulated experts, reporting only the qualitative claim that its method "can match the performance of passive querying algorithms while making a significantly lesser number of expert queries" — no specific query-count or reward numbers are reported in the paper's results, so none should be treated as given here.

**How it connects.** This paper is the theoretical capstone of §8's "when to query" question: it drops the perfect-expert assumption every other method in this section (and DAgger itself, §4) quietly relies on, and answers "how many queries are truly necessary" with a formal regret/query-complexity bound rather than an empirical heuristic — the same aspiration the Learning-to-Defer paper above has for general classification, now specific to the sequential, noisy-expert imitation-learning setting this whole lecture has been building toward.
